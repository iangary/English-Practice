## Section 2 — GRE Verbal: Reading Comprehension (timer: 8 minutes)

**Bioluminescence in the Deep Sea**

In the aphotic zone of the ocean — depths below roughly 1,000 meters,
where sunlight never penetrates — an estimated 90% of animal species
produce their own light through bioluminescence, a chemical reaction
typically involving a light-emitting molecule called luciferin and an
enzyme, luciferase, that catalyzes its oxidation. Unlike bioluminescence
in fireflies or certain fungi, which serves a single, well-understood
purpose, deep-sea bioluminescence has diversified into at least three
distinct functional categories, and a single organism may deploy more
than one of them.

The first and most intuitive use is predation: the anglerfish's
luminous lure, dangled above its jaws, exploits smaller fish's attraction
to light in an environment where food is scarce and any opportunity must
be seized. The second use inverts this logic entirely — counter-
illumination, in which animals such as certain squid produce a faint,
downward-facing glow that matches the dim light filtering from above,
erasing the silhouette that would otherwise mark them as prey to
predators hunting from below. The third use is communicative rather than
predatory: some species flash in species-specific patterns to attract
mates or signal alarm to others of their kind, a strategy that depends on
the signal being distinctive enough to avoid attracting predators that
have evolved to recognize and exploit it.

What makes the deep sea an unusual laboratory for studying this trait is
not merely its prevalence but its convergent evolution: bioluminescence
has arisen independently dozens of times across unrelated lineages,
suggesting that in an environment defined by permanent darkness, the
capacity to produce light is less a specialized adaptation than a
near-inevitable response to the selective pressures of the habitat
itself.

## Section 3 — IELTS Academic Reading (timer: 20 minutes, including answer transfer)

**Colony Collapse and the Global Pollination Crisis**

**A.** Pollinators — chiefly bees, but also moths, butterflies, and
certain birds and bats — are responsible for the reproduction of roughly
75% of the world's flowering food crops, from almonds to coffee to
apples. In the winter of 2006, beekeepers across the United States began
reporting a strange phenomenon: hives were being found largely empty,
with adult worker bees simply absent rather than dead inside the colony,
leaving the queen and immature bees behind with no apparent cause. The
media quickly termed this Colony Collapse Disorder (CCD), and although
the die-off has since eased from its early peak, honeybee populations in
many regions remain under sustained pressure from a combination of
factors rather than any single cause.

**B.** Research since 2006 has converged on four interacting stressors.
Neonicotinoid pesticides, widely used in industrial agriculture, impair
bees' navigation and memory at sub-lethal doses, making it harder for
foraging workers to find their way back to the hive. The Varroa
destructor mite, an external parasite first detected in the US in the
1980s, weakens bees directly and transmits viral pathogens between
colonies. Habitat loss has reduced the diversity of wild flowering plants
available across a full season, forcing bees fed by monoculture
agriculture into a nutritionally narrow diet for much of the year. None
of these factors alone reliably reproduces full CCD symptoms in
controlled studies, which has led most researchers to treat the
disorder as a case of cumulative stress rather than a single disease.

**C.** The economic stakes are considerable. In California's Central
Valley, the almond industry alone requires an estimated 1.8 million
honeybee colonies each February — roughly two-thirds of all managed
honeybee colonies in the United States — trucked in from across the
country for a bloom that lasts barely three weeks. Beekeepers now derive
a substantial share of their annual income not from honey but from
renting out hives for pollination services, a market that has grown
sharply as wild pollinator numbers have declined and growers have become
correspondingly more dependent on managed colonies.

**D.** Much of the public attention on CCD has focused on honeybees
specifically, but wild pollinators — bumblebees and the thousands of
solitary bee species that, unlike honeybees, do not live in hives — are
also declining, in several documented cases more severely. Because wild
pollinators are not commercially managed, their numbers are far harder
to track systematically, and researchers rely on fragmented long-term
monitoring data that varies enormously by region. Some bumblebee species
in North America and Europe have seen range contractions of over 80% in
the last half-century, a trend attributed primarily to habitat loss and
pesticide exposure rather than to any disease comparable to Varroa.

**E.** Policy responses have been uneven. The European Union imposed a
near-total ban on three major neonicotinoid pesticides in outdoor
agricultural use in 2018, following an assessment by the European Food
Safety Authority that found the chemicals posed a high risk to bees. No
equivalent federal ban exists in the United States, though several
states have passed partial restrictions. At the municipal level, a
growing number of cities have established "pollinator pathways" —
networks of native-plant gardens along urban corridors intended to give
both managed and wild pollinators continuous access to forage across an
entire season, rather than the sporadic bloom of a single crop.

## Section 5 — GRE Quant: Problem Solving (timer: 14 minutes, 1:45/question)

**1.** Pipe A fills a tank in 6 hours; pipe B drains a full tank in 10
hours. If both pipes are open on an empty tank, how many hours does it
take to fill it?

**2.** In a class, the ratio of boys to girls is 3:5. Four more boys join
the class and no girls leave or join. The ratio of boys to girls is then
1:1. How many girls are in the class?

**3.** A rectangle has a perimeter of 24 and integer side lengths. What
is the greatest possible area of the rectangle?

**4.** A set of 7 numbers has a mean of 15. One number is removed, and
the mean of the remaining 6 numbers is 14. What is the value of the
number that was removed?

**5.** In a survey of 500 people, 60% said they prefer tea and the rest
said they prefer coffee. Of the tea drinkers, 25% said they drink it
iced. How many people in the survey drink iced tea?

**6.** If 2^(x+3) = 32, what is the value of x?

**7.** A bag contains 4 red marbles and 6 blue marbles. Two marbles are
drawn at random without replacement. What is the probability that both
marbles drawn are red?

**8.** A right triangle has legs of length 9 and 12. What is the
perimeter of the triangle?

### Section 3 — IELTS Reading

5. (iii) 6. (iv) 7. (i)

### Section 5 — GRE Quant
1. 15 hours (combined rate = 1/6 − 1/10 = 1/15 per hour)
2. 10 girls (3k+4 = 5k → k = 2 → girls = 5k = 10)
3. 36 (6 × 6, the closest integer split to a square maximizes area for a
fixed perimeter)
4. 21 (sum of 7 = 105; sum of 6 = 84; removed = 105 − 84 = 21)
5. 75 people (60% of 500 = 300 tea drinkers; 25% of 300 = 75)
6. x = 2 (32 = 2^5, so x + 3 = 5)
7. 2/15 (4/10 × 3/9 = 12/90 = 2/15)
8. 36 (9-12-15 is a Pythagorean triple; perimeter = 9 + 12 + 15)
