# IELTS Listening — Practice Set 1 (All 4 Sections)

**Why this file looks different:** you can't fake real audio in a text
file, but you *can* fake the single hardest part of IELTS Listening —
**you only get to hear it once.** Protocol:

1. Set a timer. Read each transcript **one time, start to finish, at a
   normal reading pace (don't stop, don't re-read a line)**.
2. The instant you finish reading a section, cover it and answer that
   section's questions from memory — exactly like you can't rewind the
   recording.
3. Only after answering all 4 sections do you check the key.

If you catch yourself re-reading a line "just to be sure," that's not
cheating the drill, it's erasing the one skill this section trains:
catching the answer the first time it's said, because paraphrase and
distractors come before the real answer, every time.

## Section 3 — Academic discussion (multiple choice)

*Context: two students and a tutor discussing a group project.*

> **Tutor:** So, how's the research going for your renewable energy
> presentation?
> **Student A:** Good, I think — we've split it so I'm covering solar
> and Priya's covering wind, but we're a bit stuck on how to handle the
> storage problem, since neither technology works without it.
> **Student B (Priya):** Right, and the issue is most of the sources we
> found focus on *generation* capacity, not storage — which honestly
> felt misleading at first, like the articles were overselling how
> solved the problem is.
> **Tutor:** That's actually a really good critical point to raise in
> your presentation itself, not just something to grumble about in our
> meeting.
> **Student A:** Could we structure it as: here's the generation
> progress, but here's the honest gap in storage, and here's what's
> being researched to close it?
> **Tutor:** I'd go further — open with the storage gap. Starting with
> the unsolved problem is a stronger hook than ending on it, and it
> frames everything else you say as working toward solving something
> real, rather than just reporting good news.
> **Student B:** That also solves our length problem, doesn't it? We
> were worried about running over ten minutes.
> **Tutor:** It should, yes — you'll naturally cut some of the
> generation-capacity detail if it's supporting evidence rather than
> the main structure.

**Questions 11–13:** Choose the correct letter, A, B, or C.

11. What is the students' main difficulty with their current research?
    A. They cannot find enough sources on solar power.
    B. Most sources overstate how solved the storage problem is.
    C. They disagree with each other about which topic to cover.

12. What does the tutor suggest about structuring the presentation?
    A. End with the unsolved storage problem as a call to action.
    B. Open with the storage gap rather than ending with it.
    C. Remove the storage problem from the presentation entirely.

13. What does the tutor imply will happen if they restructure the talk?
    A. The presentation will likely run too short.
    B. The length problem will resolve itself naturally.
    C. They will need to add more detail on generation capacity.

## Section 4 — Academic lecture (note/sentence completion)

*Context: an introductory lecture on a historical topic.*

> "Today I want to talk about the spread of the printing press in
> fifteenth-century Europe — but not the invention itself, which you've
> already read about. I want to focus on *why* it spread so much faster
> in some regions than others, because that's the more interesting
> question. Within fifty years of Gutenberg's press, over two hundred
> European cities had their own printing operations — but that spread
> was wildly uneven. Northern Italian cities, particularly Venice,
> became printing powerhouses almost immediately, and the reason wasn't
> really about literacy rates, which were actually fairly similar across
> regions. It was about trade networks. Venice already had an extensive
> merchant shipping network moving goods — and crucially, capital — all
> over the Mediterranean and beyond. That same infrastructure, originally
> built for the spice and textile trade, could be repurposed almost
> overnight to distribute printed books. Compare that to regions with
> strong universities but weak trade infrastructure, like parts of
> rural Germany outside the main trade routes — they had plenty of
> demand from scholars, but getting the finished books out to buyers
> was slow and expensive, so printing houses there stayed small and
> few. So the lesson, if there's one lesson, is: a new technology's
> spread often depends less on the demand for it and more on whether
> existing infrastructure, built for something else entirely, happens
> to be repurposable for it."

each answer.

Spread of the printing press:

- Key factor: existing (16) ______ networks, e.g. in Venice.

- That infrastructure moved goods and, crucially, (18) ______.

### Scoring
18–20 = Band 9 · 16–17 = Band 8 · 14–15 = Band 7 · 11–13 = Band 6 ·
below 11: the problem is almost never vocabulary at this stage — it's
that you're still listening for the *exact* words in the question
instead of their paraphrase. Go back through the transcripts and
underline where a question's wording differs from the transcript's
wording. That gap is what IELTS Listening actually tests.
