# IELTS Listening — Practice Set 1 (text-based simulation)

**Why this file looks different:** real IELTS Listening is audio, played
**once**, with four sections of rising difficulty. This repo can't play
audio, so the discipline has to come from you:

**Protocol — do not skip any step:**
1. Open a text-to-speech reader (phone accessibility settings, or any
   TTS tool) and have it read each section's transcript aloud at normal
   speed — or better, have another person read it to you cold, without
   you seeing the text first. If neither is available, read it **once**,
   at a steady pace, covering the questions while you read, and only
   allow yourself to look at the questions in the 30–45 second gaps noted
   below (this is weaker than real audio practice but still beats reading
   the transcript and questions side by side, which trains a skill the
   real test doesn't reward).
2. **Never read ahead** in the transcript once you've started a section —
   the real test never lets you re-read, so neither should this.
3. You get the stated gap before each section to preview the questions
   (this mirrors real test timing), then the transcript plays/reads once.
4. Transfer answers at the end, strictly within 10 minutes for all 40
   (the real test now gives transfer time built into the section breaks
   in the computer-delivered format — simulate a single 10-minute block
   at the end here).

Total: 40 questions, difficulty rises section to section exactly like the
real test (Section 1 easiest, Section 4 hardest).

## Section 4 — Academic lecture (hardest; no second chance to catch a missed answer, by design)

*45 seconds to preview questions 31–40, then "play" the transcript once.*

> Today we're looking at the urban heat island effect — the phenomenon
> where cities are measurably warmer than their surrounding rural areas,
> sometimes by as much as 5 to 7 degrees Celsius at night. There are
> three main causes worth distinguishing. First, materials: asphalt and
> dark rooftops absorb far more solar radiation during the day than
> natural vegetation would, and release that stored heat slowly after
> sunset — this is actually the dominant factor in the *nighttime*
> temperature gap, more so than daytime heat. Second, the reduction of
> vegetation itself matters independently of surface material, because
> plants cool the air through evapotranspiration — the process by which
> water evaporates from leaves, which has a direct cooling effect similar
> to sweating. Remove the vegetation, and you remove that cooling
> mechanism entirely, not just the shade. Third, and often underestimated,
> is waste heat from human activity itself — vehicles, air conditioning
> units, and industrial processes all release heat directly into the
> urban air, and this factor becomes proportionally more significant in
> denser, more energy-intensive city centers. Now, mitigation strategies.
> Green roofs — rooftop gardens — address both the materials problem and
> the vegetation problem simultaneously, which is why they're often
> prioritized over simpler interventions like painting roofs white, even
> though reflective "cool roofs" are considerably cheaper to install.
> Cool roofs only address the materials issue, not evapotranspiration.
> Urban tree canopy expansion is the single most cost-effective
> long-term intervention per the studies we'll read this week, but it
> takes 15 to 20 years for newly planted trees to deliver their full
> cooling benefit, which creates a political problem: the city officials
> who fund the planting rarely remain in office long enough to claim
> credit for the result. One further point before we move to case
> studies — the heat island effect is not uniform across a city; it
> correlates strongly with historical housing and infrastructure
> investment patterns, meaning lower-income neighborhoods with less tree
> canopy and more paved surface often experience significantly higher
> heat exposure than wealthier neighborhoods in the very same city, which
> has become a significant environmental justice concern in recent
> public health research.

**Questions 31–40 — complete the summary. NO MORE THAN TWO WORDS for each answer.**
