# IELTS Listening — Practice Set 1

**Reality check first:** real IELTS Listening audio cannot be faithfully
reproduced in a text file — there's no substitute for actual recordings
with accents, background noise, and natural pacing. Two ways to use this
file honestly:

1. **Best:** paste a section's transcript into a text-to-speech tool (or
   have someone else read it to you) at natural conversational speed, play
   it **exactly once**, and answer while listening — no replays, no pausing.
2. **Minimum viable:** read each transcript yourself, once, at normal
   reading speed, covering the questions until you finish reading. Then
   answer from memory before looking back. This is weaker than real audio
   practice but still trains note-taking-while-processing far better than
   reading the questions first and hunting for answers (which is not how
   the exam works).

**Timer:** 30 minutes for all four sections below + questions, no stopping
between sections (there is no break in the real exam), then a hard stop —
whatever you haven't answered, leave blank, exactly as you would on test day.

## Section 3 — Academic discussion (multiple choice)

**Context:** Two students, Marcus and Elena, discuss a group project with
their tutor.

> **Tutor:** So how's the research going for the group presentation?
> **Marcus:** Honestly, we've hit a snag. Elena and I disagree on the
> structure — I think we should present our findings first and the
> methodology second, since that's more engaging for the audience.
> **Elena:** I see the appeal, but I think that's backwards for an academic
> audience specifically. If they don't understand how we got the data,
> the findings won't mean much to them.
> **Tutor:** You're both making reasonable points, but for this particular
> assignment, remember the marking criteria explicitly reward a clear,
> conventional structure — methodology, then findings, then discussion.
> Deviating from that risks confusing the markers more than it engages them.
> **Marcus:** Fair enough, that settles it then.
> **Tutor:** The bigger issue I noticed in your draft is that your sample
> size section is quite thin — you mention the number of participants but
> not how you recruited them, and a marker will want to know whether
> there's selection bias.
> **Elena:** We can expand that section — we recruited through the
> university's psychology participant pool, so there's actually a good
> answer there, we just didn't write it down.
> **Tutor:** Good, add that in. One last thing: your submission deadline
> got moved — it's now the end of next week, not this Friday, because of
> the public holiday.

**Questions 13–17: Choose the correct letter, A, B, or C.**

13. What do Marcus and Elena initially disagree about?
    A. which topic to research
    B. the order of sections in the presentation
    C. who should present which section

14. Why does the tutor say the methodology should come first?
    A. It is required by the department's formatting rules.
    B. The marking criteria reward a conventional structure.
    C. Marcus's preferred order was factually incorrect.

15. What problem does the tutor identify in the draft?
    A. The findings section is too long.
    B. The sample size section lacks detail about recruitment.
    C. The group has too few participants.

16. How does Elena respond to this problem?
    A. She admits the information doesn't exist yet.
    B. She explains the missing information was simply left out.
    C. She disagrees that it's a real problem.

17. What has changed about the deadline?
    A. It has moved earlier because of a public holiday.
    B. It has moved later because of a public holiday.
    C. It has not changed, despite a public holiday.

## Section 4 — Academic lecture (summary completion)

**Context:** A lecture excerpt on coral reef ecosystems.

> Today I want to talk about coral bleaching, which is one of the clearest
> indicators we have of ocean stress from climate change. Corals are
> animals that live in a symbiotic relationship with algae called
> zooxanthellae, which live in the coral's tissue and provide it with food
> through photosynthesis — in exchange, the coral gives the algae shelter
> and the compounds they need to photosynthesize. This relationship is
> also what gives healthy coral its vivid color, since the pigments mostly
> come from the algae, not the coral itself. When water temperatures rise
> even slightly above the coral's normal tolerance — sometimes by as
> little as one degree Celsius sustained over several weeks — the coral
> experiences stress and expels the algae living in its tissue. Without
> the algae, the coral loses both its food source and its color, turning
> white, which is why we call this bleaching. Bleached coral is not
> immediately dead; if temperatures return to normal quickly enough, the
> coral can recruit new algae and recover. But if the stress continues,
> the coral eventually starves and dies, and the physical reef structure
> it built up over centuries begins to erode, taking with it the habitat
> for the enormous diversity of fish and invertebrate species that depend
> on reef structure for shelter. Recovery, when it happens at all, can
> take over a decade, which is far slower than the rate at which
> bleaching events are now recurring in many parts of the world.

**Questions 18–23: Complete the summary. Write ONE WORD ONLY for each answer.**

Coral gets its color mainly from algae called zooxanthellae living in its
(18) ______. The algae provide the coral with (19) ______ through
photosynthesis, while the coral provides the algae with shelter. When
water becomes too (20) ______ for a sustained period, the coral expels the
algae and turns white — a process called (21) ______. If conditions
improve quickly, the coral can (22) ______; if stress continues, the coral
dies and the reef (23) ______ over time.

### Scoring

This set has 23 questions, not the real exam's 40, so treat it as a pacing
and technique drill rather than a literal band predictor. As a rough
guide: 21–23 correct = strong (exam-ready technique), 17–20 = solid but
review which section type cost you points, below 17 = identify whether
you're losing points to **mishearing** (wrong word) or **missing the
window** (the answer was said while you were still writing the previous
one) — these need different fixes. The second one means you need to
practice writing faster and trusting short-form notes, not relistening.

choice/summary), and the specific cause in `PROGRESS_LOG.md`.
