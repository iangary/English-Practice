# IELTS Listening — Practice Set 1 (Sections 1 & 4)

**Why this file looks different from a normal script:** you can't replay
the real recording, so don't replay this one either. Read each transcript
below **silently, once, at a normal reading pace** (don't slow down to
"catch" answers — that's not how the audio will behave), jotting notes as
you go exactly as you would while listening. Only look at the questions
*after* you finish reading each transcript once. This is a deliberately
uncomfortable constraint — that discomfort is the actual skill being
trained.

**Full timing:** 30 minutes listening + 10 minutes transfer, for the full
4-section test. This set covers 2 of the 4 sections (20 of 40 questions) —
cap yourself at **12 minutes total** (read once, answer, transfer) to keep
proportional pressure.

## Section 4 — Academic lecture (monologue, note completion)

Context: a lecture excerpt on bioluminescence. Read once, then answer
Q11–20.

> "Today I want to look at bioluminescence — the production of light by
> living organisms — because it's a good example of a trait that evolved
> independently many times rather than just once from a shared ancestor.
> Estimates vary, but researchers have identified at least 40 separate
> evolutionary origins of bioluminescence across different groups of
> organisms, from bacteria to fish to fungi.
>
> The chemistry itself is broadly similar across these cases: a molecule
> called luciferin reacts with oxygen, a reaction catalysed by an enzyme
> called luciferase, releasing energy as light rather than heat — which is
> why it's sometimes called 'cold light.' In fact, biological light
> production is remarkably efficient: nearly all of the energy released
> becomes light, compared with an incandescent bulb, where only around 10%
> of energy becomes light and the rest is wasted as heat.
>
> Marine environments show the widest range of uses. In the deep ocean,
> where sunlight doesn't penetrate past roughly 1,000 metres, some species
> use bioluminescence for camouflage — a technique called counter-
> illumination, where an animal produces light on its underside matching
> the faint light from above, making its silhouette invisible to predators
> looking up from below. Other species use it the opposite way, as a
> warning signal, flashing brightly when disturbed to startle a predator
> or, in some cases, to attract an even larger predator that will then
> attack whatever was threatening them — a strategy researchers sometimes
> call a 'burglar alarm' effect.
>
> On land, the most familiar example is the firefly, which uses
> bioluminescence primarily for mate attraction, with each species
> producing a distinctive flash pattern — essentially a species-specific
> code — so that females can identify compatible males in the dark.
> Interestingly, some firefly females have evolved the ability to mimic
> the flash pattern of a different species in order to lure in and prey on
> males who approach expecting to find a mate — so in this one trait,
> we see it used simultaneously for communication, attraction, predation,
> and defence, depending on the organism.
>
> Current research interest isn't just evolutionary, though — the
> luciferase enzyme itself has become a major tool in laboratory science,
> used as what's called a 'reporter gene,' allowing researchers to
> literally see, via light output, when and where a particular gene is
> being activated inside living cells, which has applications in cancer
> research and drug development."

### Scoring and common traps to notice

18–20/20 = Band 9 pace · 15–17 = Band 8 · 12–14 = Band 7 · below 12:
go back and identify *where* the trap was — this set deliberately includes
the three most common IELTS Section 1/4 traps:

- **Self-correction** (Q3's phone number, Q9's original date): the first
  number stated is wrong and corrected a few seconds later. Real test
  audio does this constantly — the answer is always the **final, corrected**
  value, never the first one mentioned.
- **Spelling under pressure** (Q2): a name is spelled, partially
  misheard/repeated incorrectly by the other speaker, then corrected. If
  you wrote "Kowalcyk" you fell for exactly the trap this was designed to
  test.
- **Distractor numbers close together** (Q6/Q7): the standard price and
  discounted price are both stated — if you wrote 180 for Q7, you grabbed
  the first number instead of tracking which field the question actually
  asked for.

Log your score and which trap type (if any) caught you in
`PROGRESS_LOG.md` — "I missed a number" is not specific enough to fix;
"I take the first-stated number instead of waiting for the correction" is.
