# IELTS Academic Listening — Practice Test 1

**Timer: 30 minutes for the four sections below + 10 minutes to transfer
answers to a clean answer sheet. Do not pause between sections — the real
test doesn't.**

## How to run this without audio

You don't have a recording, so simulate the one-pass condition that makes
Listening hard: have someone else read each script aloud to you at a
natural pace **once**, or read it aloud into a voice memo and play it back
**once**, no pausing, no rewinding. If you read it silently to yourself
you are not practicing Listening, you are practicing Reading with extra
steps — the entire skill being tested is extracting answers from a single
pass at natural speaking speed. Give yourself the question-reading time
noted before each section (this mirrors the real test's preview pauses),
then "play" the script.

## Section 1 — Conversation (questions 1–10)

*Context: a student, Maya, is enquiring about renting a room through a
university accommodation office.*

**Preview time: 30 seconds.** Look at questions 1–10 below before the
script "plays."

**Script:**

OFFICER: Good morning, Student Accommodation Office, how can I help?

MAYA: Hi, I'm looking for a room for next semester — I'm a postgraduate
student starting in January.

OFFICER: Sure, let's get your details. Can I take your full name first?

MAYA: It's Maya Petrova — P-E-T-R-O-V-A.

OFFICER: Great, and what's your student ID number?

MAYA: It's 7-7-3-4-9-2-1.

OFFICER: Thanks. Now, we have three types of rooms available for January:
a studio at £195 a week, a shared flat at £140 a week, and a single room
in a residence hall at £165 a week. The studio's actually fully booked
now, so really it's between the shared flat and the hall room.

MAYA: I'd prefer not to share a kitchen, so maybe the hall room.

OFFICER: Good choice — that one includes a weekly cleaning service too,
which the shared flat doesn't.

MAYA: Oh that's useful. Is there a deposit?

OFFICER: Yes, the deposit is £250, refundable at the end of the tenancy
provided there's no damage. That's separate from the weekly rent.

MAYA: And how do I pay the weekly rent — monthly in advance?

OFFICER: Yes, in advance, by bank transfer only — we don't accept cash or
card for rent payments.

MAYA: Got it. One more thing — is the hall close to the Engineering
building? That's where most of my classes are.

OFFICER: It's on Dalton Road, about a twelve-minute walk from Engineering.
There's also a bike shelter on site if that helps.

MAYA: That's fine, I'll walk. What do I need to bring on move-in day?

OFFICER: Just photo ID and proof of enrollment — we'll issue your key and
a welcome pack with the fire safety information in it.

MAYA: And when exactly can I move in?

OFFICER: The hall opens for new residents on January the 6th, from 10am.

MAYA: Perfect, thank you so much.

## Section 2 — Monologue (questions 11–20)

*Context: a guide giving an orientation talk to new visitors at a
botanical garden.*

**Preview time: 30 seconds.**

**Script:**

Welcome to Fairview Botanical Gardens. Before we start the tour, let me
give you a quick overview of the site, because it's bigger than most
people expect — about 40 hectares in total.

If you entered through the Main Gate, you're currently standing in the
Visitor Centre, which also houses our café and a small gift shop. From
here, the Rose Garden is the closest major feature, just two minutes'
walk to the east. Please note the Rose Garden is closed to the public
on Tuesdays for maintenance — every other day it's open from 9 till 5.

Heading north from the Visitor Centre takes you to the Glasshouse, home
to our tropical collection. That one requires a separate ticket — £4 for
adults, free for under-12s — because of the climate control costs. The
Glasshouse is the only part of the garden that's fully wheelchair
accessible via a ramp at the north entrance; the Rose Garden and the
Woodland Trail both have some gravel paths that can be difficult.

If you continue further north past the Glasshouse you'll reach the
Woodland Trail, a 3-kilometre loop through native woodland. It typically
takes about an hour to walk at a relaxed pace. Please stay on the marked
path — we've had problems recently with visitors disturbing ground-
nesting birds in the undergrowth.

For lunch, the café by the Visitor Centre serves hot food until 2:30pm,
but there's also a picnic area beside the lake, to the west of the Rose
Garden, which is open all day and is a nicer spot if the weather's good.

One last thing — if you're a member rather than a day visitor, members
get free entry to the Glasshouse and a 10% discount at the gift shop,
but that discount doesn't apply to the café.

Right, let's begin the tour at the Rose Garden.

## Section 3 — Discussion between students (questions 21–30)

*Context: two students, Tom and Priya, discuss a group project on
renewable energy with their tutor, Dr. Ahmed.*

**Preview time: 45 seconds (this section has harder question types).**

**Script:**

DR. AHMED: So, Tom, Priya — how's the renewable energy project coming
along? You were looking at comparing solar and wind, is that right?

PRIYA: Yes, we split it so I'm covering solar and Tom's covering wind,
but we realized we also need a section comparing their costs directly,
which neither of us had planned for.

TOM: Right, and that's turned out to be harder than expected because the
cost data varies so much by country — we can't just use one global figure.

DR. AHMED: That's a fair complication. What's your plan for handling it?

PRIYA: We're thinking of focusing on just three countries — maybe
Germany, India, and Australia — since they each represent a different
stage of renewable adoption.

DR. AHMED: That's sensible, though I'd actually suggest swapping Australia
for Denmark. Australia's grid data is harder to access reliably, and
Denmark's wind sector gives you a cleaner comparison point.

TOM: Okay, we can do that. Priya, does that change your solar section much?

PRIYA: Not really, I'll just adjust the comparison table.

DR. AHMED: Good. Now, in terms of structure — I'd recommend you don't
present solar and wind as two separate halves of the report. Weave the
cost comparison through both sections instead, so the reader isn't
waiting until the end to see how they stack up against each other.

TOM: That makes sense, though it probably means restructuring what I've
already written.

DR. AHMED: Worth it, I think. One more thing: your proposal mentioned
using a 2019 report as your main cost source. I'd actually avoid that —
costs have shifted so much since then that it'll undermine your argument
to a marker who knows the field. Use something from the last two years
instead.

PRIYA: We can find an updated source, that's not a problem.

DR. AHMED: Great. Last point — the deadline. It's officially the 15th,
but I'd strongly suggest submitting a draft to me by the 8th so I can
flag anything major before you're locked in.

TOM: We'll aim for the 8th then.

**Questions 21–30 (multiple choice — circle one letter):**

    (A) policy comparison (B) cost comparison (C) environmental impact

    (A) data is outdated (B) data varies heavily by country (C) data is confidential

    (A) Germany (B) India (C) Australia

    (A) Denmark (B) Japan (C) Brazil

    (A) political instability (B) unreliable grid data access (C) low solar output

27. Dr. Ahmed recommends the report structure should:
    (A) keep solar and wind fully separate (B) weave cost comparison through both sections (C) put cost comparison in an appendix

28. What's wrong with the 2019 report as a source?
    (A) it's not peer-reviewed (B) costs have changed significantly since then (C) it doesn't cover wind energy

29. The official deadline is the:
    (A) 8th (B) 15th (C) 21st

    (A) submit the final report (B) submit a draft (C) book a meeting

## Section 4 — Academic lecture (questions 31–40)

*Context: part of a lecture on the history of coral reef research.*

**Preview time: 45 seconds.**

**Script:**

Today I want to cover three major shifts in how scientists have studied
coral reefs since the early 20th century, because the methods used in
each era shaped what questions researchers were even able to ask.

The first era, running roughly from 1900 to the 1950s, was defined by
what we might call descriptive naturalism. Researchers like the
expedition teams who studied the Great Barrier Reef in 1928 and 1929
worked almost entirely by direct observation — diving with basic
equipment, cataloguing species, and mapping reef structures by hand. The
key limitation here wasn't a lack of curiosity, it was technology: without
reliable underwater breathing apparatus, most observation happened in
shallow water only, meaning deep reef systems remained essentially
unknown for decades.

That changed in the second era, from the 1950s through the 1980s, with
the widespread adoption of SCUBA diving equipment. This opened up much
deeper and more extensive survey work, and it's during this period that
researchers first began systematically documenting coral bleaching
events, although at the time bleaching was generally assumed to be a
localized, recoverable phenomenon rather than a symptom of a broader
climate pattern. It's worth noting that funding during this era came
overwhelmingly from fisheries-focused agencies, which meant research
priorities skewed toward commercially relevant species rather than reef
ecosystems as a whole.

The third era, from the 1990s onward, is characterized by remote sensing
and genetic analysis. Satellite imagery now allows researchers to track
reef health across entire ocean basins rather than single sites, while
genetic sequencing has revealed that what were once classified as single
coral species are frequently complexes of genetically distinct types with
different heat tolerances — a discovery with real significance for
predicting which reefs might survive rising ocean temperatures.

Interestingly, this third era also saw a shift in who's doing the
research. Citizen science programs, where recreational divers log
observations through standardized apps, now contribute a meaningful
fraction of the global monitoring data — something that would have been
unthinkable to the expedition scientists of the 1920s, who treated reef
observation as a highly specialized, credentialed activity.

Looking ahead, the next frontier is likely to be automated monitoring:
fixed underwater sensor networks and AI-based image classification that
can track reef condition continuously, without requiring a diver in the
water at all.

33. What equipment became widespread in the second era (1950s–1980s)? __________
34. During the second era, coral bleaching was assumed to be: __________
35. Which type of agency mostly funded second-era research? __________
36. What two methods define the third era (1990s onward)? __________ and __________
37. What did genetic sequencing reveal about coral species classification? __________
38. Why does this genetic discovery matter for climate predictions? __________
39. Who contributes data through citizen science programs? __________
40. What is named as the likely "next frontier" of reef monitoring? __________

## Self-grading

- **39–40 correct:** Band 9 territory. Your bottleneck now is Speaking
  and Writing, not Listening.
- **35–38:** Band 8. Review every miss — was it a word you didn't catch,
  or a word you heard but mis-mapped to the wrong question?
- **30–34:** Band 7. Section 3 (discussion, multiple choice) is the
  classic Band 7 ceiling — the answers require tracking who-said-what
  across a conversation, not just spotting keywords.
- **Below 30:** Don't re-read the script silently and re-grade yourself
  "knowing" the answers. Re-run the whole test, one pass, no pausing,

  performance is visible.
