## The Overfishing of the Deep Sea

**A.** For most of human history, the deep ocean — generally defined as
waters below 200 meters, where sunlight no longer penetrates — was
effectively immune to fishing pressure, protected not by policy but by
inaccessibility. This changed rapidly from the 1960s onward as advances in
sonar, synthetic fiber nets, and diesel-powered trawling made it
commercially feasible to fish at depths exceeding 1,000 meters. Species
that had evolved in an environment with almost no historical predation
pressure from humans were, within a few decades, subjected to industrial
harvesting rates, with little preceding research into whether their
populations could sustain such pressure.

**B.** The biological characteristics of deep-sea species make this a
particularly poor match. Many, such as the orange roughy, are
extraordinarily long-lived — individuals have been aged at over 100 years
— and correspondingly slow to reproduce, often not reaching sexual
maturity until 20 to 30 years of age. This life-history strategy, suited
to a stable deep-sea environment with low natural mortality, leaves these
populations almost incapable of recovering from the kind of rapid,
high-volume removal that commercial trawling applies. A shallow-water
species that reproduces annually and reaches maturity within a year or
two can rebuild a depleted population within a decade under reduced
fishing pressure; an orange roughy population fished down by 80% may
require a century or more to recover, if it recovers at all, since there
is no guarantee the removed biomass will be replaced before further
fishing pressure resumes.

**C.** Bottom trawling, the dominant method used to harvest deep-sea
fish, compounds the problem beyond simple overharvesting. Heavy nets
dragged across the seafloor destroy cold-water coral reefs and sponge
fields that can themselves be thousands of years old and that provide
essential habitat structure for numerous other species. Unlike their
shallow-water, tropical counterparts, cold-water corals grow at a rate of
only millimeters per year, meaning that physical damage from a single
trawling pass can represent the destruction of centuries of accumulated
growth, with recovery timescales measured in centuries rather than years.
Some marine ecologists have argued that this habitat destruction, rather
than the direct removal of target fish, represents the more severe and
less reversible impact of deep-sea trawling.

**D.** Economically, deep-sea trawling has long depended on government
subsidies to remain viable at all. Multiple studies have found that, once
fuel subsidies are removed from the calculation, a substantial share of
the global deep-sea trawling fleet would operate at a net financial loss,
given the high fuel costs of reaching and operating at depth relative to
the modest catch volumes typically achieved. This has led some economists
to characterize deep-sea trawling not as a naturally profitable industry
being regulated, but as an artificially sustained one whose true costs —
both financial, once subsidies are stripped out, and ecological, once
habitat destruction is counted — have historically been borne by parties
other than the fleets themselves.

**E.** Policy responses have been uneven. Several regional fisheries
management organizations have introduced depth limits on trawling or
established marine protected areas over known cold-water coral habitats,
and the United Nations has called on member states to assess the impact
of bottom trawling before authorizing it in new areas. However,
enforcement in international waters beyond any single nation's
jurisdiction remains difficult, and some researchers argue that
voluntary commitments, without independent verification or meaningful
penalties, have had limited measurable effect on total trawling effort in
practice. Others counter that even imperfect measures have likely slowed,
if not reversed, the rate of new habitat destruction in the areas they
cover, and that treating partial progress as equivalent to no progress
risks undermining support for further incremental measures.

**F.** The broader lesson some researchers draw is that fisheries
management frameworks developed for fast-reproducing, shallow-water
species are poorly suited, almost by design, to the deep sea, and that
applying them without modification has produced a pattern in which
regulation consistently arrives after a population has already been
substantially depleted rather than before. Whether this pattern can be
reversed for the deep sea's slowest-reproducing species, several of which
may now be fished at levels from which full recovery within a human
lifetime is no longer possible even under a complete fishing moratorium,
remains, by the admission of researchers in the field, genuinely
uncertain.

## Answer Key

1. **TRUE** — "effectively immune to fishing pressure... until the 1960s."
2. **TRUE** — "often not reaching sexual maturity until 20 to 30 years of age" vs. "a year or two" for shallow-water species.
3. **FALSE** — the passage says a shallow-water species "*can* rebuild... within a decade," not that it *always* does — watch the absolute wording the question adds that the passage doesn't support.
4. **FALSE** — cold-water corals grow "only millimeters per year," implied slower, and the passage contrasts them with tropical corals as "their... counterparts," implying the opposite of "faster."
5. **TRUE** — "a substantial share... would operate at a net financial loss" once subsidies are removed. ("Most" is a close paraphrase of "a substantial share" here, but note this is a borderline call — re-read paragraph D carefully if you missed it.)
6. **FALSE** — "several," not "all," regional fisheries management organizations.
7. **TRUE** — "may now be fished at levels from which full recovery... is no longer possible even under a complete fishing moratorium."
