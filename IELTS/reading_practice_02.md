## The Replication Crisis in Science

**A.** In 2015, a large collaborative project known as the Open Science
Collaboration attempted to replicate the results of 100 psychology
studies published in top journals. The project found that only around
36% of the replications produced a statistically significant result
matching the original finding, despite following the original methods
as closely as possible. This finding, published in the journal
*Science*, crystallized a concern that had been building for several
years and came to be known as the "replication crisis." The crisis had
an earlier, more theatrical trigger: a 2011 paper by psychologist Daryl
Bem claiming experimental evidence for precognition — the ability to
sense future events — used entirely conventional statistical methods,
yet reached a conclusion most scientists considered implausible on its
face. If standard methods could produce apparent evidence for
precognition, critics argued, something was likely wrong with how those
methods were being applied across the field, not merely with this one
study.

**B.** Several interacting factors were identified as contributing
causes. "P-hacking" — the practice, often unconscious, of analyzing
data in multiple ways until a statistically significant result emerges,
then reporting only that analysis — was found to be widespread even
among well-intentioned researchers who did not consider it misconduct.
Journals compounded the problem through publication bias: a dramatic,
novel, or counterintuitive positive finding was far more likely to be
accepted for publication than a null result, creating an incentive
structure that rewarded striking results over careful, cautious ones.
Small sample sizes, common in psychology due to the cost and difficulty
of recruiting participants, further meant that many "significant"
findings were likely to be statistical flukes that would not recur
under a second round of testing.

**C.** Psychology was not alone. A 2012 study by the biotechnology
researchers C. Glenn Begley and Lee Ellis attempted to confirm the
findings of 53 "landmark" cancer biology studies and succeeded in
replicating only six. Given that a substantial share of pharmaceutical
drug development depends on exactly this kind of foundational
laboratory research, the implications extended well beyond academic
embarrassment: resources were potentially being directed toward drug
candidates built on findings that could not be reliably reproduced.
Economics, too, faced its own reckoning, with several influential
empirical papers failing to replicate once other researchers gained
access to the original data and code.

**D.** In response, the scientific community has converged on several
proposed reforms. Pre-registration requires researchers to publicly
specify their hypotheses and planned statistical analyses before
collecting data, closing off the option to quietly change the analysis
after seeing the results. "Registered reports," a publishing format
adopted by a growing number of journals, take this further by having
journals accept or reject a study based on its methodological quality
before the results are even known, removing publication bias at its
root. Other proposed fixes include mandating larger sample sizes,
requiring researchers to share raw data and analysis code openly, and
training students explicitly in the statistical pitfalls that produce
false positives.

**E.** Not every researcher agrees on how the crisis should be
interpreted, however. Some caution that a failed replication does not
automatically prove the original finding was false; it may instead
reflect an unidentified "hidden moderator" — some contextual factor
present in the original study but absent, perhaps unknowingly, from the
replication attempt. Others go further, arguing that the reform
movement, in its enthusiasm for caution, risks discouraging the kind of
small-sample, exploratory research that has historically generated the
hypotheses later confirmed by larger, more rigorous studies; a science
that only rewards pre-registered confirmation, these critics suggest,
may struggle to generate genuinely new ideas in the first place.

**F.** Beyond the scientific community itself, the replication crisis
has consequences for how the public understands and trusts scientific
findings. Media coverage has often amplified single, unreplicated
studies as if they were settled fact, creating a cycle in which the
public is later told to disregard conclusions it had been encouraged to
accept uncritically only months before. Some commentators have called
for a cultural shift toward "slow science" — a willingness to withhold
confident public claims until findings have been independently
confirmed — arguing that the long-term credibility of science depends
less on the speed of discovery than on the reliability of what is
eventually reported as established.

## Answer Key

1. **FALSE** — only around 36% replicated, not a majority.
2. **FALSE** — the paper "used entirely conventional statistical
   methods"; the problem was the implication for the field, not the
   methods themselves.
3. **FALSE** — p-hacking was "often unconscious" and researchers who did
   it "did not consider it misconduct."
4. **TRUE** — only six of 53 "landmark" studies were replicated.
5. **NOT GIVEN** — the passage mentions economics faced "its own
   reckoning" with some papers failing to replicate, but says nothing
   about universal adoption of registered reports there.
6. **E**
7. **C**
