## The Bystander Effect Revisited

**A.** In 1964, the murder of Kitty Genovese in New York City became, in
the retelling, a parable about urban indifference: newspaper accounts
claimed that dozens of neighbors heard her screams and did nothing. The
story, though later shown to be substantially exaggerated by the original
reporting, spurred psychologists John Darley and Bibb Latané to formulate
what became known as the bystander effect — the finding that an
individual's likelihood of intervening in an emergency decreases as the
number of other bystanders present increases. Their experiments, in which
participants believed they were one of several people overhearing a
simulated seizure, found that help was offered more slowly, and less
often, when subjects believed other bystanders were also listening.

**B.** The dominant explanation for decades centered on two related
mechanisms. The first, diffusion of responsibility, holds that each
bystander feels less personally obligated to act because responsibility
is shared among everyone present. The second, pluralistic ignorance,
describes a situation in which bystanders look to others' reactions to
gauge whether a situation is truly an emergency, and because everyone is
simultaneously looking to everyone else for a cue while maintaining a
calm outward appearance, the group collectively misreads a genuine
emergency as a non-event. Both mechanisms were treated for years as
largely unconscious processes, operating beneath deliberate reasoning.

**C.** More recent research has complicated this tidy account. A 2011
meta-analysis examining decades of bystander studies found that the
effect, while real on average, is substantially weaker in situations
perceived as dangerous to the bystander and in situations where
bystanders know one another, as opposed to being strangers. In contexts
such as physical assaults, where the cost of inaction could be severe and
visible, group size showed little or no suppressive effect on
intervention — suggesting that the original laboratory paradigm, built
around ambiguous, low-stakes scenarios, may have overstated how
universally the effect applies outside such conditions.

**D.** A separate line of research has drawn on surveillance footage
rather than laboratory simulations, allowing researchers to study actual
public conflicts rather than staged ones. A 2020 study analyzing camera
footage of real altercations across multiple countries found that
intervention by at least one bystander occurred in the large majority of
cases, and that larger crowds were, if anything, slightly more likely to
produce an intervention than smaller ones — the near-opposite of the
classic laboratory prediction. The researchers proposed that real crowds,
unlike strangers brought together briefly in a lab, often contain at
least one person predisposed to act, and that a single intervention can
rapidly cascade as others join once the ambiguity is resolved by the
first mover.

**E.** These findings have not overturned the bystander effect so much as
narrowed its domain. Reviewers of this newer evidence emphasize that
laboratory studies and real-world footage are not actually measuring the
same thing: laboratory paradigms typically present an emergency whose
severity is deliberately ambiguous, isolate participants from one
another so that the dynamics of cascading intervention cannot occur, and
remove the social cost of being seen to do nothing by people who know
you. Real-world public conflicts, by contrast, are often unambiguous,
allow one bystander's action to visibly trigger others, and frequently
occur among at least partially acquainted individuals. Rather than
asking "does the bystander effect exist," the more useful question, these
researchers argue, is "under which specific conditions does added group
size help, hurt, or do nothing to the odds that someone intervenes."

**F.** The practical implications extend into safety training and public
health campaigns. Programs that once taught the public simply to
"overcome the bystander effect" by appealing to individual conscience are
increasingly being redesigned to teach specific, concrete actions —
such as pointing at one identified bystander and directly assigning them
a task ("you, call emergency services") — a technique designed to defeat
diffusion of responsibility directly by removing the ambiguity about who
is responsible, rather than relying on raising general awareness of the
phenomenon itself.

### Why this passage is harder than Passage 1
Matching Headings is consistently the lowest-scoring question type across
IELTS test-takers because it requires holding the *argument* of an entire
paragraph in mind, not locating one fact. If you missed more than one
heading, re-read the paragraph and write its argument in your own words in
one sentence *before* looking at the options again — do not pattern-match
on repeated keywords between the paragraph and the heading, which is the
trap the wrong options are built on.
