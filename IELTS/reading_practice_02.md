## The Jevons Paradox

**A.** In 1865, the English economist William Stanley Jevons observed
something that seemed, at first glance, to contradict common sense: as
steam engines became more efficient at converting coal into useful work,
Britain's total coal consumption rose rather than fell. Jevons argued that
this was not a coincidence but a predictable consequence of efficiency
gains themselves. When a resource is used more efficiently, the effective
cost of using it drops, which in turn increases demand for the activities
that rely on it—often by enough to outweigh the saving from efficiency
and push total consumption higher, not lower. This phenomenon later came
to bear his name: the Jevons paradox.

**B.** For over a century, the Jevons paradox remained a largely
theoretical curiosity, invoked mainly by economic historians discussing
nineteenth-century industry. It resurfaced with new urgency in debates
over modern energy policy, as governments and corporations promoted
efficiency improvements—more fuel-efficient vehicles, more efficient
lighting, better-insulated buildings—as a primary strategy for reducing
overall energy consumption and associated emissions. Critics pointed to
the Jevons paradox as a reason for skepticism: if historical precedent
held, efficiency gains alone might fail to reduce consumption and could,
in some cases, increase it, as cheaper-to-run technology encouraged more
intensive use.

**C.** Economists have since refined Jevons's original, fairly absolute
claim into a more nuanced concept known as the "rebound effect," which
describes the proportion of expected energy savings that is offset by
increased consumption following an efficiency improvement, without
necessarily claiming that consumption rises overall. A rebound effect of
100% or more—termed "backfire"—corresponds to Jevons's original paradox,
in which efficiency gains are entirely or more than entirely offset.
Empirical estimates of rebound effects vary enormously by sector: studies
of household heating and vehicle fuel efficiency generally find rebound
effects in the range of 10–30%, meaning most, but not all, of the
efficiency saving is retained as a net reduction, while some
energy-intensive industrial processes have shown rebound effects
approaching or exceeding 100% under certain conditions.

**D.** The size of the rebound effect appears to depend heavily on how
central the resource is to overall spending and on whether cheaper access
unlocks genuinely new uses rather than merely cheaper versions of
existing ones. Lighting provides a striking historical illustration: as
the cost of producing a given amount of light fell dramatically from
tallow candles to gas lighting to electric bulbs to LEDs, humanity did
not merely light existing spaces more cheaply—it began lighting spaces
and for durations that would previously have been considered
extravagant, from illuminated advertising to stadiums lit for nighttime
events. Economic historians estimate that global per-capita consumption
of artificial light has increased by many orders of magnitude even as the
cost per unit of light has fallen by a comparable amount, a pattern
consistent with rebound effects at or near the "backfire" end of the
spectrum for this particular case.

**E.** None of this implies that efficiency improvements are pointless.
Even where a rebound effect is substantial, consumers typically gain
real value—more light, more mobility, more comfort—that would not
otherwise have existed at the previous cost, which is itself a form of
economic benefit even if it complicates simple emissions-reduction
accounting. The policy implication favored by most economists studying
the issue is not to abandon efficiency standards but to pair them with
mechanisms that prevent the cost savings from fully translating into
cheaper resource use—carbon pricing being the most commonly proposed
example—so that efficiency gains translate more reliably into reduced
total consumption rather than simply cheaper, more abundant consumption.

**F.** This debate illustrates a broader caution relevant well beyond
energy policy: interventions aimed at reducing the use of a resource by
making that use more efficient can fail, or even backfire, if they
ignore how the resulting cost reduction changes behavior. The same
logical structure has been applied, with mixed empirical support, to
domains including water conservation technology, computing power, and
even road capacity, where added lanes intended to ease congestion have in
some studies been found to induce enough additional driving to leave
congestion largely unchanged within a few years—a close cousin of the
Jevons paradox sometimes called "induced demand."
