# GRE Verbal — High-Frequency Vocabulary, Set 2 (30 words)

**Gate before you start:** do not open this set until you can drill all
30 words in `verbal_vocab.md` both directions (word→meaning, meaning→word)
in under 3 seconds each, twice in a row, on different days. These words
are rarer and get confused with each other far more easily than Set 1 —
starting here without Set 1 solid just builds a shakier foundation faster.

**Drill protocol:** same as Set 1 — word → meaning, then meaning → word,
cover the side you're not using. Several of these words are commonly
confused with a near-opposite or near-synonym; the "Watch the trap"
column calls out the specific word test-writers use to bait a wrong
answer on Sentence Equivalence.

| Word | Part of speech | Definition | Example sentence | Watch the trap |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Anodyne | adj./n. | inoffensive, bland; (n.) something that soothes pain | The spokesperson gave an **anodyne** response that said nothing of substance. | Confused with "innocuous" (similar) vs. "caustic" (near-opposite). |
| Bellicose | adj. | eager to fight; aggressively hostile | The senator's **bellicose** rhetoric alarmed even his own party. | Confused with "belligerent" (true synonym) vs. "placid" (opposite). |
| Circumlocution | n. | an indirect, wordy way of saying something | His answer was pure **circumlocution** — ten minutes to avoid a yes or no. | Don't confuse with "eloquence"; circumlocution is evasive, not skillful. |
| Dilettante | n. | someone with a superficial, amateur interest in a subject | He called himself a photographer, but his peers saw him as a **dilettante**. | Contrast with "virtuoso" — opposite level of mastery, same subject area. |
| Effrontery | n. | shameless boldness; audacity | She had the **effrontery** to demand a refund after breaking the item herself. | Near-synonym "temerity" is close but implies more recklessness than shamelessness. |
| Fecund | adj. | highly fertile; intellectually productive | The workshop proved remarkably **fecund**, producing a dozen publishable ideas. | Don't confuse with "fetid" (foul-smelling) — a classic sound-alike trap. |
| Gregarious | adj. | sociable; fond of company | Her **gregarious** nature made her the obvious choice to host the reunion. | Opposite of "reclusive"/"taciturn" — test writers pair these as a contrast set. |
| Harangue | n./v. | a lengthy, aggressive lecture or tirade | The coach's halftime **harangue** left no doubt about his frustration. | Stronger than "admonish" — harangue implies length and forcefulness, not a brief warning. |
| Impecunious | adj. | having little or no money | The **impecunious** artist survived on grant money and borrowed studio space. | Confused with "impetuous" (reckless) — unrelated meaning, similar spelling. |
| Jejune | adj. | naive, simplistic, or lacking nutritional/intellectual substance | Critics dismissed the essay as **jejune**, offering no insight past the obvious. | Doesn't mean "young" despite the French root feel — means intellectually thin. |
| Kowtow | v. | to act in an excessively submissive manner | Employees were expected to **kowtow** to the founder's every demand. | Implies excessive, often humiliating deference — stronger than "defer to." |
| Loquacious | adj. | very talkative | The **loquacious** tour guide covered three centuries of history before lunch. | Near-synonym of "garrulous" (Set 1) but loquacious is more neutral/positive in tone. |
| Mendacious | adj. | lying; untruthful | The **mendacious** testimony fell apart under cross-examination. | Confused with "mendicant" (a beggar) — unrelated, similar spelling. |
| Nebulous | adj. | vague, unclear, lacking definite form | The proposal's **nebulous** goals made it impossible to measure success. | Don't confuse with "nascent" (Set 1, means "just beginning") — different dimension entirely. |
| Obsequious | adj. | excessively eager to please or obey | The **obsequious** waiter hovered anxiously after every bite. | Stronger/more negative than "deferential" — implies insincerity. |
| Perfunctory | adj. | done with minimal effort or reflection, as a mere formality | He gave a **perfunctory** apology and immediately changed the subject. | Confused with "cursory" (close synonym, fine) vs. "thorough" (opposite). |
| Querulous | adj. | habitually complaining; peevish | The **querulous** passenger found fault with every seat he was offered. | Don't confuse with "quizzical" (puzzled) — similar sound, unrelated meaning. |
| Recondite | adj. | obscure, abstruse; understood by only a few | The footnote referenced a **recondite** branch of 17th-century law. | Near-synonym of "abstruse" (Set 1) — GRE sometimes pairs these as the correct double-blank. |
| Sycophant | n. | a person who flatters others to gain advantage | The CEO surrounded himself with **sycophants** who never challenged his ideas. | Related to "obsequious" (behavior) — sycophant is the person, obsequious describes the manner. |
| Truncate | v. | to shorten by cutting off part | The editor had to **truncate** the article to fit the print layout. | Don't confuse with "truncated" meaning merely "short" — it implies something was cut off. |
| Umbrage | n. | offense; resentment | She took **umbrage** at the implication that her work was careless. | Fixed phrase "take umbrage" — rarely appears outside that collocation. |
| Vindicate | v. | to clear of blame; to prove right | New evidence eventually **vindicated** the scientist's controversial theory. | Don't confuse with "vindictive" (seeking revenge) — opposite emotional register. |
| Wanton | adj. | deliberate and unprovoked; reckless | The report described **wanton** disregard for basic safety procedures. | Carries a judgmental tone of excess/cruelty, stronger than "careless." |
| Xenophobic | adj. | fearful or hateful of foreigners/strangers | The **xenophobic** policy drew condemnation from international observers. | Pairs with "cosmopolitan" as an opposite in Sentence Equivalence contrast structures. |
| Yoke | v./n. | to join or link together, often restrictively | The two proposals were **yoked** together in a single vote, forcing an all-or-nothing choice. | Verb sense (to link) is the one GRE tests, not just the farm-equipment noun. |
| Zealous | adj. | marked by great energy or enthusiasm for a cause | Her **zealous** defense of the policy surprised colleagues who knew her as cautious. | Less extreme than "fanatical"; don't over-read it as negative — it can be neutral/positive. |
| Abnegate | v. | to renounce or give up a right/belief | He chose to **abnegate** his claim to the inheritance rather than fight his siblings in court. | Confused with "abrogate" (to abolish a law/agreement) — different object, similar sound. |
| Abrogate | v. | to formally abolish or repeal (a law, right, agreement) | The new government moved to **abrogate** the previous administration's trade agreements. | See above — keep abnegate (personal renunciation) distinct from abrogate (formal repeal). |
| Contrite | adj. | feeling or showing genuine remorse | His **contrite** apology, offered without being asked, restored some trust. | Stronger than "apologetic" — implies deep, sincere guilt, not just politeness. |
| Diffident | adj. | lacking confidence; shy about asserting oneself | Her **diffident** manner in meetings masked genuinely sharp analysis. | Opposite of "audacious"/"brash" — a classic GRE antonym pairing. |

## Drill: fill in the blank (cover the answer key below)

1. Rather than taking a clear position, the memo was full of ______,
   never actually committing to a recommendation.
2. The intern's ______ attitude toward senior staff bordered on
   embarrassing, agreeing with every remark before it was even finished.
3. Despite decades of research, the phenomenon remains ______, resisting
   every attempt at a clear explanation.
4. He accepted the committee's harsh critique with a surprisingly
   ______ response, admitting the project's flaws without defensiveness.
5. The new regulation would ______ three existing trade agreements at
   once, effectively starting the entire framework over.

### Answer key
1. circumlocution
2. obsequious / sycophantic
3. recondite
4. contrite
5. abrogate

## Why this set is harder than it looks

Set 1 tests whether you know the word. Set 2 increasingly tests whether
you can tell two *related* words apart under time pressure — mendacious
vs. mendicant, abnegate vs. abrogate, jejune vs. "young." On the real
exam, wrong answers are built from exactly this kind of near-miss, not
from a totally unrelated word. When you self-grade the drill above, don't
just check if you got the "right" word — check whether you can state in
one sentence *why* the trap word was wrong.
