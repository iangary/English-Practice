# GRE Reading Comprehension — Practice Passage 1

**Timer: 8 minutes for this passage and its 6 questions — GRE RC rewards
reading for structure/argument on the first pass, not memorizing detail,

## Passage

Economists have long debated whether minimum wage increases cause
measurable job losses, a question that might seem straightforwardly
empirical but has proven remarkably resistant to consensus. Classical
economic theory predicts a clear mechanism: if the price of labor rises
above its market equilibrium, employers will demand less of it, resulting
in reduced employment, particularly among low-skill workers. This
prediction follows directly from the standard supply-and-demand model
taught in introductory economics, and for decades it was treated as
settled fact among most economists.

That consensus was disrupted by a 1994 study examining fast-food
employment in New Jersey after the state raised its minimum wage, using
neighboring Pennsylvania—where wages remained unchanged—as a comparison.
Contrary to the classical prediction, the researchers found no evidence
of job losses in New Jersey relative to Pennsylvania; if anything,
employment grew slightly. The study ignited substantial controversy, not
only over its conclusions but over its methodology, with critics arguing
that comparing two adjacent states, however similar, could not adequately
control for all relevant economic differences, and that the study's
reliance on telephone surveys of individual restaurants introduced data
quality concerns that payroll-record-based studies would not share.

Subsequent research using payroll data rather than phone surveys produced
mixed results, with some studies supporting the original finding of
minimal employment effects and others finding modest negative effects
concentrated specifically among the lowest-wage, least-experienced
workers. A particularly influential strand of more recent research has
argued that the effect of a minimum wage increase depends heavily on how
large the increase is relative to the local labor market: a modest
increase in a high-wage urban market may have negligible employment
effects, while a comparably large increase imposed on a low-wage rural
market may price out a meaningful share of low-productivity jobs. This
framing suggests that decades of seemingly contradictory findings were
not necessarily measuring different things badly, but were accurately
measuring genuinely different economic situations.

This reframing carries a practical implication that has only recently
begun to influence policy: rather than debating a single "correct"
federal minimum wage, some economists now argue for wage floors indexed
to local labor market conditions, such as regional median wages, so that
the same nominal policy does not function as a modest adjustment in one
region and a severe shock in another. Critics of this approach counter
that regionally indexed minimum wages would be considerably more complex
to administer and could create perceived unfairness among workers in
different regions performing identical jobs, a political cost that may
outweigh the economic precision gained.

## Questions

**1. Main idea:** Which best describes the primary purpose of the passage?
(A) To argue that minimum wage increases always reduce employment
(B) To trace how empirical research complicated a once-settled economic
    prediction and to describe an emerging reframing of the debate
(C) To prove that the 1994 New Jersey study was methodologically flawed
    beyond use
(D) To advocate specifically for a federal minimum wage increase

**2. Detail:** According to the passage, what was one specific
methodological criticism of the 1994 study?
(A) It only examined one industry over too short a time period.
(B) It relied on telephone surveys rather than payroll records.
(C) It was conducted after the minimum wage increase had been reversed.
(D) It failed to include any comparison state at all.

**3. Inference:** It can be inferred that subsequent payroll-data studies
(A) uniformly confirmed the original 1994 study's findings
(B) uniformly contradicted the original 1994 study's findings
(C) produced varied results that a later theory attempted to explain
    through local labor market conditions
(D) were considered less reliable than phone-survey-based studies

**4. Function:** Why does the author mention that "a modest increase in
a high-wage urban market may have negligible employment effects, while a
comparably large increase...may price out...low-productivity jobs"?
(A) To introduce a new criticism of all prior research on the topic
(B) To illustrate the specific mechanism behind the reframing described
    in that paragraph
(C) To argue that urban minimum wages should always be higher than rural
    ones
(D) To contradict the classical economic prediction entirely

**5. Detail:** According to the final paragraph, what is one argument
against regionally indexed minimum wages?
(A) They would reduce employment more than a uniform federal minimum wage.
(B) They are more complex to administer and could create a sense of
    unfairness between workers in different regions.
(C) They have never been proposed by any economist.
(D) They would eliminate the minimum wage entirely.

**6. Tone/attitude:** The author's overall tone toward the debate
described in the passage is best characterized as
(A) dismissive of empirical economic research
(B) strongly advocating for one side of the policy debate
(C) measured and analytical, presenting the evolution of the debate
    without endorsing a single conclusion
(D) openly mocking economists who hold the classical view

## Answer Key

1. **B** 2. **B** 3. **C** 4. **B** 5. **B** 6. **C**
