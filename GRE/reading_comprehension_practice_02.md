## Passage 1 (short)

Philosophers of science have long distinguished between the *context of
discovery*—the psychological or historical process by which a scientist
arrives at a hypothesis—and the *context of justification*—the logical
process by which that hypothesis is tested and either supported or
refuted by evidence. This distinction implies that the origin of an idea,
however accidental or intuitive, is irrelevant to its scientific
validity; what matters is whether the idea survives rigorous testing.
Some recent historians of science have challenged this neat separation,
arguing that the two contexts are not so easily disentangled in practice.
They point out that the very choice of which hypotheses are deemed worth
testing—and which experiments are designed to test them—is itself shaped
by the same background assumptions, disciplinary biases, and even
aesthetic preferences that influenced the hypothesis's discovery in the
first place. On this view, justification is never a purely neutral
filter applied after the fact; it is already partly shaped by the
conditions of discovery it is supposed to be independent from.

## Passage 2 (long)

For much of the twentieth century, the dominant model of memory treated
it as analogous to a filing system: experiences were "encoded" and
stored in discrete locations, to be "retrieved" largely intact when
needed, with forgetting understood as either decay of the stored trace or
failure to locate it. This model, while intuitive, struggled to account
for a growing body of evidence showing that memories are not simply
retrieved but actively reconstructed at the moment of recall, drawing on
both the original experience and information acquired afterward. The
most striking demonstrations of this came from studies in which
subjects, exposed to subtly misleading information after witnessing an
event, later recalled the misleading detail as part of the original
event itself, often with high confidence and no awareness that their
memory had been altered. Such findings suggested that memory is less
like a video recording retrieved on demand and more like a story
reassembled each time it is told, with each retelling susceptible to
revision.

This reconstructive view has since become the mainstream position among
memory researchers, but it has generated its own interpretive
complications. If every act of recall is also, in some sense, an act of
re-encoding, then the distinction between an "original" memory and a
"distorted" one becomes harder to maintain in any strict sense—there may
be no pristine original to compare a later recollection against, only a
chain of progressively reconstructed versions. Some researchers have
embraced this implication fully, arguing that questions like "is this
memory accurate?" are often less useful than questions like "how and why
did this memory change?" Others worry that pushing the reconstructive
view too far risks an unhelpful relativism, in which no memory can be
meaningfully called more or less accurate than another—a conclusion that
sits uneasily with practical contexts, such as courtroom eyewitness
testimony, where the accuracy of a specific memory of a specific event
genuinely matters and cannot be waved away as "just another
reconstruction."

A middle position, increasingly common in the field, holds that
reconstruction and accuracy are not opposites: a memory can be
actively reconstructed at each recall AND still be substantially faithful
to the original event, provided the reconstructive process draws
primarily on robust original encoding rather than on post-event
information. On this view, the practical question is not whether
reconstruction happens—it always does—but what inputs the
reconstruction draws on each time, which is a question that can, in
principle, still be investigated empirically rather than dissolved into
philosophical relativism.

### Notes on the formats that trip people up

- **EXCEPT questions (Q2, Q6):** Read these as "which one is NOT
  supported" — the trap is that three plausible-sounding answers ARE
  supported, and under time pressure people stop at the first plausible
  one instead of checking all four. Always check all four on EXCEPT
  questions, no exceptions (so to speak).
- **Select-All-That-Apply (Q3, Q8):** No partial credit on the real test
  — getting 2 of 3 correct answers (and missing the third, or including a
  wrong one) scores zero. Evaluate each option independently as true/
  false against the passage; don't compare options against each other.
- **Select-a-Sentence (Q1):** The trap is picking the sentence that's
  topically related rather than the one that states the specific claim
  being asked about. Re-read the question stem twice before scanning.
