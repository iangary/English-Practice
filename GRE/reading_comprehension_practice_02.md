## Passage

The replication crisis that has roiled experimental psychology since the
early 2010s is frequently narrated as a story of fraud or sloppiness, but
this framing obscures a more unsettling structural problem: many
published findings may have been generated by entirely honest researchers
following entirely conventional statistical practice. The culprit, on
this account, is not dishonesty but a set of incentives and procedures—
collectively termed "researcher degrees of freedom"—that permit a
researcher to make numerous small, individually defensible decisions
(which outliers to exclude, which covariates to control for, when to stop
collecting data) that, in aggregate, substantially inflate the likelihood
of finding a statistically significant result even when no true effect
exists.

This diagnosis reframes the crisis as one of *methodology* rather than
*character*, with an important consequence: pre-registration, the
practice of publicly committing to a study's hypotheses and analysis plan
before data collection begins, has emerged as the leading proposed
remedy, precisely because it removes the researcher's freedom to make
those decisions after seeing the data. Early evidence appeared to vindicate
this approach—pre-registered studies in psychology show dramatically
lower rates of statistically significant findings than non-pre-registered
studies on comparable topics, which proponents interpret as evidence that
pre-registration successfully filters out inflated, false-positive
results.

A more skeptical reading of the same evidence, however, notes that this
comparison conflates two distinct effects that are difficult to
disentangle: pre-registration may indeed suppress false positives, but it
may also simply discourage researchers from attempting riskier,
higher-variance hypotheses in the first place, precisely because a null
result from a pre-registered study cannot be quietly set aside and
reframed as an exploratory finding. If this latter effect is
substantial, the lower significance rate among pre-registered studies
would partly reflect a more conservative *selection* of which questions
get asked, not merely a cleaner *test* of the questions that are asked—a
distinction with real consequences for whether pre-registration should be
treated as a near-universal requirement or as one tool suited
particularly to confirmatory, high-stakes research.

A further complication is that pre-registration, whatever its statistical
merits, imposes a real cost on exploratory science, the process by which
researchers notice unexpected patterns in data and generate new
hypotheses worth testing later. A discipline that over-corrects toward
pre-registration risks mistaking rigor for the whole of scientific
practice, forgetting that confirmatory testing is only valuable because
some prior, necessarily less rigorous process generated the hypothesis
being confirmed.

## Questions

**1. Main idea:** The passage is primarily concerned with
(A) proving that pre-registration eliminates the replication crisis
(B) arguing that researcher fraud is more widespread than previously
    believed
(C) examining both the rationale for pre-registration as a remedy and a
    complication in interpreting the evidence for its effectiveness
(D) dismissing pre-registration as an ineffective and costly practice

**2. Detail:** According to the passage, "researcher degrees of freedom"
refers to
(A) a researcher's freedom to choose any research topic they wish
(B) small, individually defensible analytical decisions that can
    collectively inflate false-positive rates
(C) a formal statistical test used to detect fraud
(D) the number of participants required for a valid study

**3. Inference:** The author most likely mentions that pre-registration
removes "the researcher's freedom to make those decisions after seeing
the data" in order to
(A) criticize pre-registration as overly restrictive
(B) explain the specific mechanism by which pre-registration is expected
    to reduce inflated findings
(C) argue that researchers should be trusted to make these decisions
    themselves
(D) suggest that data collection itself is the main source of error

**4. Function:** The third paragraph (beginning "A more skeptical
reading...") primarily functions to
(A) refute the claim that pre-registration has any effect on findings
(B) introduce an alternative explanation for the same observed data that
    complicates a straightforward causal interpretation
(C) provide additional evidence that pre-registration eliminates fraud
(D) summarize the history of the replication crisis

**5. Inference:** It can be inferred that a "null result from a
pre-registered study cannot be quietly set aside and reframed as an
exploratory finding" because
(A) journals refuse to publish any pre-registered studies
(B) the public commitment to a hypothesis before data collection makes
    it evident if the original, unsuccessful hypothesis is later
    abandoned without disclosure
(C) null results are always more interesting than significant ones
(D) pre-registration is legally binding

**6. Detail:** According to the final paragraph, over-reliance on
pre-registration risks
(A) increasing the rate of outright scientific fraud
(B) undervaluing the exploratory process that generates hypotheses worth
    later confirming
(C) making all psychological research impossible to publish
(D) eliminating the need for statistical analysis entirely

**7. Tone/attitude:** The author's attitude toward pre-registration as
presented in the passage is best described as
(A) uncritically enthusiastic
(B) entirely dismissive
(C) measured — acknowledging its rationale while complicating a simple
    narrative of its effectiveness and flagging a real tradeoff
(D) indifferent to the topic

## Answer Key

1. **C** 2. **B** 3. **B** 4. **B** 5. **B** 6. **B** 7. **C**
