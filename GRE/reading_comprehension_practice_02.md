## Passage

Historians of science have frequently treated the Copernican
revolution as a straightforward narrative of empirical triumph: Nicolaus
Copernicus proposed that the Earth orbits the Sun, the heliocentric
model was eventually vindicated by observation, and geocentrism was
discarded as an outdated superstition. This account, however, elides a
significant complication that troubled astronomers for nearly a
century after Copernicus's 1543 publication: the heliocentric model, as
Copernicus himself formulated it, was not demonstrably more accurate
than the geocentric model it was meant to replace. Copernicus retained
the ancient assumption that celestial orbits must be perfect circles,
an assumption inherited from Aristotelian cosmology rather than derived
from observation, and this constraint forced him to retain a
cumbersome system of auxiliary circles (epicycles) nearly as complex as
the Ptolemaic system he sought to supplant. Predictive accuracy, by the
most relevant contemporary measure, offered observers little reason to
prefer one system over the other.

What did, eventually, distinguish heliocentrism was not a single
decisive observation but a series of developments across the following
century that addressed different weaknesses in turn. Johannes Kepler's
discovery that planetary orbits are elliptical rather than circular,
published in 1609, eliminated the need for epicycles and dramatically
simplified the heliocentric model's mathematics, finally giving it a
genuine predictive edge. Galileo's telescopic observations beginning in
1610—including the phases of Venus, which are incompatible with simple
geocentric models, and the moons of Jupiter, which demonstrated that
not all celestial bodies orbit the Earth—provided observational
evidence that, while not strictly disproving all geocentric variants,
substantially narrowed the range of models that remained observationally
viable. Finally, Isaac Newton's 1687 formulation of universal
gravitation supplied a physical mechanism explaining *why* planets
would move as Kepler's laws described, converting heliocentrism from a
mathematically convenient description into a physically necessitated
one.

This drawn-out sequence complicates any simple story in which
observation alone forces a theory's abandonment. For nearly a century,
working astronomers who continued to use geocentric or quasi-geocentric
models, such as Tycho Brahe's system in which other planets orbited the
Sun while the Sun itself orbited a stationary Earth, were not thereby
being irrational or anti-empirical; Brahe's system, notably, was
consistent with the observational evidence available during his
lifetime and avoided a problem that troubled early heliocentrism—the
absence of any observable stellar parallax, which a moving Earth should
in principle produce, but which the vast distances to stars (not
confirmed until the nineteenth century) placed far beyond the
resolution of contemporary instruments. Viewed this way, the gradual
acceptance of heliocentrism reflects less a single triumph of reason
over dogma than an extended process in which successive technical
problems were solved one at a time, each removing a legitimate
objection rather than any one of them alone proving decisive.

## Questions

**1. Main idea:** Which best states the primary purpose of the passage?
(A) To argue that Copernicus's model was entirely without merit
(B) To show that the acceptance of heliocentrism resulted from a
    sequence of separate developments resolving distinct objections,
    rather than from a single decisive proof
(C) To prove that Tycho Brahe's system was ultimately correct
(D) To argue that Galileo's observations alone settled the debate

**2. Detail:** According to the passage, what limitation did
Copernicus's original model share with the Ptolemaic system it was
meant to replace?
(A) Both relied on a stationary Sun at the center.
(B) Both retained circular orbits and required epicycles as a result.
(C) Both were based entirely on telescopic observation.
(D) Both predicted the phases of Venus incorrectly.

**3. Function:** Why does the author mention the absence of observable
stellar parallax?
(A) To prove that the Earth does not actually move

(C) To argue that nineteenth-century astronomers finally disproved
    heliocentrism
(D) To show that Brahe's instruments were more advanced than Galileo's

**4. Inference:** It can be inferred that the author regards

(B) reasonably responding to the observational evidence available to

**5. Detail:** According to the passage, what was the specific

(B) It introduced elliptical orbits, removing the need for epicycles

(C) It supplied a physical mechanism (gravitation) explaining planetary
    motion.
(D) It proved the existence of stellar parallax.

**6. Organization:** How is the second paragraph of the passage
primarily organized?
