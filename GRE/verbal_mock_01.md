## Reading Comprehension — Passage 1 (Questions 12–15)

Timer guidance: ~4 minutes including questions.

Behavioral economists have long documented the "endowment effect," the
tendency for people to value an item more highly simply because they own
it, often demanding a price to sell an item well above what they would
pay to acquire the identical item if they did not already own it.
Early explanations treated this gap as evidence of pure loss aversion: a
rational actor experiences a potential sale as a loss, and because losses
are felt more acutely than equivalent gains, sellers demand a premium to
accept that loss. This account, drawn directly from prospect theory,
dominated the literature for roughly two decades following its initial
documentation.

A competing line of research has since argued that loss aversion alone
cannot fully explain the effect, pointing to experiments in which the
endowment effect appears even when subjects merely imagine owning an
item, or when ownership is purely nominal and temporary, conditions under
which a pure loss-aversion account predicts little or no gap. These
researchers instead propose that the effect partly reflects an
identity-based attachment: once an item is associated with the self, even
briefly, parting with it is processed not merely as a financial loss but
as a small loss of self, a mechanism distinct from—though potentially
compounding—ordinary loss aversion. Critics of this identity-based
account counter that many of the experiments supporting it still involve
enough ambiguity about genuine versus nominal ownership that loss
aversion cannot be fully ruled out as the sole mechanism, leaving the
debate unresolved.

## Reading Comprehension — Passage 2 (Questions 16–20)

Timer guidance: ~5 minutes including questions.

The "paradox of thrift," first popularized by John Maynard Keynes,
describes a scenario in which an increase in individual saving, though
rational and beneficial for any single household, can reduce aggregate
demand enough to shrink the overall economy, potentially leaving even
diligent savers worse off than if everyone had continued spending at
prior levels. The mechanism is straightforward at the level of aggregate
demand: one person's spending is another person's income, so a
widespread shift toward saving reduces the income of businesses and
workers alike, which can in turn reduce the very income from which future
saving would otherwise come.

The paradox, however, is not universally accepted as applying under all
conditions. Critics, particularly those drawing on classical and
neoclassical traditions, argue that the mechanism depends heavily on
prices and interest rates failing to adjust: if interest rates fall in
response to increased saving, the theory goes, that additional saving
should be channeled into increased investment, offsetting the drop in
consumption rather than simply vanishing from the economy. Keynesians
respond that in practice, particularly during periods of already low
interest rates or financial crisis, interest rates may have little room
to fall further, or firms may decline to invest even cheap capital given
pessimistic expectations about future demand—a scenario sometimes
described as a "liquidity trap"—meaning the offsetting investment the
classical model predicts may simply fail to materialize when it is
needed most. Empirical resolution of the debate has proven difficult,
since real economies rarely offer a clean natural experiment isolating
savings behavior from the many other factors that simultaneously
influence aggregate demand.
