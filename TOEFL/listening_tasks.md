# TOEFL Listening — Practice Set 1 (Conversation + Lecture)

**Protocol — this matters more here than anywhere else in this repo:**
read each transcript below **exactly once, straight through, at normal
reading speed, taking notes as you go** (structure — who/what/why/how
ideas connect — not transcription). Then cover the transcript and
answer the questions using only your notes. Re-reading the transcript
before answering defeats the entire point of the drill: on the real

## Part 1: Campus Conversation

*Context: a student talking to a university librarian.*

> **Student:** Hi, sorry — I got an email saying I have a hold on my
> account because of an overdue book, but I'm pretty sure I returned
> it weeks ago.
> **Librarian:** Let me pull that up... Okay, I see it. The system
> shows it was checked out but there's no return scan on file. When do
> you think you returned it?
> **Student:** It would've been right before spring break, so, early
> March. I used the drop box outside, the one by the east entrance,
> because the library was already closed.
> **Librarian:** Ah — that explains it, actually. That particular drop
> box has had a known issue where books get jammed near the opening and
> don't fall all the way into the bin, so they never get scanned in
> until someone does a manual check, which only happens every couple of
> weeks. I'm not saying that's definitely what happened, but it's common
> enough with that box that we don't automatically charge replacement
> fees when a student says they used it.
> **Student:** Oh, that's a relief. So what do I need to do now?
> **Librarian:** I'll flag your account as "disputed — pending box
> check" rather than removing the hold outright, since I can't confirm
> it without physically checking. That removes the late fee
> accumulation immediately, though. If the book turns up in the box
> check, which happens this Friday, the hold clears automatically and
> you won't need to do anything else.
> **Student:** And if it doesn't turn up?
> **Librarian:** Then we'd ask you to either locate your copy or cover
> the replacement cost, but given how often this exact box causes this
> exact problem, I'd genuinely be surprised if that's where this ends
> up.

**Questions 1–6**

1. Why does the student visit the librarian?
   (A) To request a book be held for pickup
   (B) To dispute a hold placed on his account
   (C) To ask about library opening hours
   (D) To return a book in person

2. What does the librarian imply about the east entrance drop box?
   (A) It has been permanently removed from service.
   (B) It frequently fails to register returned books correctly.
   (C) It is only for use during the day.
   (D) It is reserved for a different library branch.

3. What is the immediate effect of the librarian flagging the account
   as "disputed"?
   (A) The book is marked as officially returned.
   (B) The late fee stops accumulating right away.
   (C) The student is charged a reduced replacement fee.
   (D) The account hold is removed permanently.

4. **Inference:** What can be inferred about the librarian's attitude
   toward the student's account of events?
   (A) She is skeptical and believes he is likely mistaken.
   (B) She finds his explanation plausible given past experience with
       that drop box.
   (C) She is required to believe him regardless of the circumstances.
   (D) She thinks he should have used a different drop box.

5. What will happen if the book is found during Friday's box check?
   (A) The student will still need to pay a partial fee.
   (B) The hold will clear without further action from the student.
   (C) The student will need to resubmit a dispute form.
   (D) The librarian will contact the student's academic advisor.

6. **Function:** Why does the librarian say, "I'm not saying that's
   definitely what happened"?
   (A) To accuse the student of being dishonest
   (B) To qualify her explanation as a likely, not certain, cause
   (C) To change the subject to a different policy
   (D) To indicate the book is permanently lost

## Part 2: Academic Lecture (Biology)

*Context: an introductory lecture on animal behavior.*

> "Let's talk about a behavior that looks irrational at first glance
> but turns out to make a lot of evolutionary sense: alarm calling.
> You'd think that if a predator shows up, the smart move for an
> individual animal is to stay silent and let someone else get caught.
> Calling out seems to do nothing but draw the predator's attention to
> *you*, the caller. And yet alarm calls are extremely common across
> social species — ground squirrels, vervet monkeys, many birds. So why
> hasn't natural selection eliminated such a seemingly self-destructive
> behavior?
>
> The classic explanation is kin selection. In many of these species,
> alarm calls are given overwhelmingly by individuals living near close
> relatives — siblings, offspring, parents. Belding's ground squirrels
> are the textbook case here: female ground squirrels, who typically
> live their whole lives near their female relatives, give alarm calls
> far more often than males, who disperse away from their birth
> colony and end up surrounded by non-relatives. From a pure
> individual-survival standpoint, calling looks like a loss. But from a
> genetic standpoint — remember, you share genes with close relatives —
> helping several relatives survive can outweigh the personal risk, in
> terms of how many of your genes make it to the next generation.
>
> Now, kin selection isn't the whole story, and this is the part that
> often gets left out of simplified versions of this topic. Alarm
> calling also persists in some situations where the caller has no
> close relatives nearby at all. One competing explanation here is the
> 'perception advertisement' hypothesis — the idea that calling signals
> to the predator, 'I've seen you, there's no point stalking me
> specifically,' which can actually reduce the caller's own risk rather
> than increase it, if predators preferentially pursue prey that
> haven't noticed them. So in reality, most researchers now think
> alarm calling is maintained by a combination of these pressures, with
> the relative importance of each depending heavily on the specific
> species and its social structure."

**Questions 7–12**

7. What is the main purpose of the lecture?
   (A) To argue that alarm calling has no evolutionary explanation
   (B) To explain why a seemingly self-destructive behavior persists
       evolutionarily
   (C) To compare ground squirrels and vervet monkeys in detail
   (D) To describe how predators choose their prey

8. According to the lecture, why do female Belding's ground squirrels
   give alarm calls more often than males?
   (A) Females are naturally less cautious than males.
   (B) Females typically live near close relatives; males disperse.
   (C) Males are physically unable to produce the alarm call.
   (D) Females are targeted less often by predators.

9. **Function:** Why does the professor mention the "perception
   advertisement" hypothesis?
   (A) To completely replace the kin selection explanation
   (B) To introduce an additional explanation for cases kin selection
       doesn't fully cover
   (C) To criticize the methodology of ground squirrel studies
   (D) To argue that alarm calls don't actually warn other animals

10. According to the "perception advertisement" hypothesis, why might
    calling out actually reduce risk to the caller?
    (A) It confuses the predator about the caller's location.
    (B) Predators are more likely to pursue prey that hasn't noticed
        them, so signaling awareness can deter pursuit.
    (C) It alerts other prey animals to attack the predator together.
    (D) It causes the predator to leave the area entirely.

11. What is the professor's overall conclusion about the cause of
    alarm calling?
    (A) Kin selection is the only valid explanation.
    (B) It is most likely maintained by a combination of pressures,
        varying by species.
    (C) The behavior remains a complete evolutionary mystery.
    (D) Perception advertisement has been disproven.

12. **Organization:** How does the professor structure the
    explanation of alarm calling?
    (A) Presents one theory, then introduces a competing or
        complementary theory, then synthesizes both.
    (B) Presents three unrelated theories with no comparison.
    (C) Describes the behavior without offering any explanation.
    (D) Moves chronologically through the history of the research.
