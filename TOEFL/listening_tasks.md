# TOEFL Listening — Drill Set

**Reality check first:** like IELTS Listening, this can't fully replace
real recorded audio with natural speech patterns and length (real TOEFL
lectures run 3–5 minutes, conversations 2–3 minutes). Use text-to-speech
or have someone read these aloud, once, at natural speed, while you
practice the note-taking method below. Reading it yourself once, without
looking ahead at the questions, is the fallback.

## Note-taking method: structure, not transcription

The single biggest fix for most people's Listening score has nothing to
do with English ability — it's trying to write down too much. You cannot
transcribe a lecture and listen at the same time; something will be
missed, and it's usually the connections between ideas, which is exactly
what gets tested. Instead, capture:

- **The main claim or topic** (one line, as soon as it's stated)
- **Each new example or sub-point**, abbreviated (arrows, dashes, not
  full sentences)
- **Signal words** that mark structure: *"however," "the key difference
  is," "for example," "as a result," "in contrast"* — these tell you
  where a question is likely to come from
- **Numbers, names, and dates** exactly — these are the one category
  worth writing in full, since you can't reconstruct them from context

## Lecture — Introductory Psychology (6 questions)

> Today we're going to talk about something called the bystander effect.
> This is the finding that an individual is actually *less* likely to
> help a person in distress when other people are present, compared to
> when they're alone — which is counterintuitive, right? You'd think more
> witnesses would mean more help available, not less. The classic
> explanation involves two mechanisms working together. The first is
> diffusion of responsibility — when multiple people are present, each
> individual feels less personally obligated to act, because the
> responsibility is shared, or at least feels shared, among everyone
> present. The second is what's called social influence, or sometimes
> pluralistic ignorance — in an ambiguous emergency, people look to
> others' reactions to figure out how to interpret the situation, and if
> everyone else appears calm, or is similarly looking around rather than
> acting, each person concludes the situation must not actually be an
> emergency, even if privately they're uncertain. This was studied
> famously after a 1964 case in New York where a woman was attacked
> while, according to later-disputed reports at the time, numerous
> neighbors reportedly did nothing — and while the exact details of that
> specific case turned out to be more complicated than initial reporting
> suggested, the phenomenon itself has been replicated extensively in
> controlled experiments since then. One of the most interesting findings
> from later research is that simply knowing about the bystander effect
> measurably increases the likelihood that someone will intervene the
> next time they witness an ambiguous emergency — in other words,
> awareness of the bias is itself a partial antidote to the bias, which
> isn't true of most cognitive biases we discuss in this course.

**Questions:**

1. What is the main topic of the lecture?
   A. A specific 1964 crime case
   B. Why people help strangers more in groups
   C. Why individuals are less likely to help when others are present
   D. How to train people to overcome fear in emergencies

2. According to the professor, what is "diffusion of responsibility"?
   A. Responsibility is shared, so each individual feels less obligated
      to act
   B. People become confused about who is actually in danger
   C. Groups dilute the severity of the emergency
   D. Responsibility shifts entirely to the first person who arrives

3. What role does "pluralistic ignorance" play, according to the lecture?
   A. People assume others know more about the emergency than they do
   B. People use others' apparent calm to conclude there's no real emergency
   C. People ignore the situation because they don't understand it
   D. People wait for official authorities before acting

4. Why does the professor mention the 1964 New York case with a caveat
   about "disputed reports"?
   A. To argue the bystander effect isn't real
   B. To acknowledge the specific case's details were more complex than
      originally reported, while noting the broader phenomenon still holds
   C. To criticize the researchers who studied it
   D. To provide the exact year the theory was first proposed

5. What does the professor say about simply knowing about the bystander effect?
   A. It has no measurable impact on behavior
   B. It makes people more anxious in emergencies
   C. It measurably increases the likelihood of intervening later
   D. It only works for trained psychologists

6. What is implied by the professor's comparison to "most cognitive biases
   we discuss in this course"?
   A. Most biases can also be reduced simply by learning about them
   B. Most biases, unlike this one, are not reduced simply by awareness
   C. This course mostly covers biases related to emergencies
   D. Cognitive biases are generally easy to overcome

## Conversation — Student and academic advisor (5 questions)

> **Advisor:** Come in — I saw your email about changing your course
> schedule. What's going on?
> **Student:** Thanks for fitting me in. So, I'm currently registered for
> Organic Chemistry and Statistics this semester, but I just found out my
> part-time job moved my shifts, and now they directly conflict with the
> Statistics lecture time.
> **Advisor:** I see. Is there another section of Statistics offered at a
> different time?
> **Student:** There is one, but it's already full, and the waitlist has
> about fifteen people ahead of me, so that's probably not realistic this
> semester.
> **Advisor:** Okay. In that case, you have two options as I see it. You
> could drop Statistics this semester and pick it up next semester
> instead, or you could switch to the online asynchronous version of the
> course, which doesn't meet at a fixed time at all.
> **Student:** I didn't know there was an online version. Is it taught by
> the same professor?
> **Advisor:** No, actually — it's taught by a different instructor, Dr.
> Reyes, but it covers identical material and counts the same toward your
> requirement. The main difference is you'd submit weekly problem sets on
> your own schedule rather than attending a lecture.
> **Student:** That actually sounds like it could work better for me
> anyway, given my job. Are there any downsides I should think about?
> **Advisor:** The main one students mention is that it requires more
> self-discipline — there's no fixed meeting time to force you to keep
> pace, so people who struggle with that tend to fall behind closer to
> exams. But academically, there's no disadvantage on your transcript or
> your GPA calculation.
> **Student:** Okay, I think I'd like to switch to the online section,
> then.
> **Advisor:** Great, I'll process that today. You'll get a confirmation
> email once it's done, probably by tomorrow.

**Questions:**

7. Why does the student want to change their schedule?
   A. They are failing Organic Chemistry
   B. Their job shift now conflicts with their Statistics lecture
   C. They want a lighter course load
   D. They dislike their current Statistics professor

8. Why doesn't the student switch to the other in-person Statistics section?
   A. It's taught by an instructor with a bad reputation
   B. It conflicts with Organic Chemistry as well
   C. It's full, with a long waitlist
   D. It meets too early in the morning

9. What is one difference between the online section and the in-person one?
   A. It is taught by a different professor
   B. It does not count toward the degree requirement
   C. It covers less material
   D. It costs additional tuition

10. What downside does the advisor mention about the online option?
    A. It has a worse average grade distribution
    B. It requires more self-discipline without a fixed meeting time
    C. It is not available to all students
    D. It does not offer the same credit hours

11. What does the student decide to do?
    A. Drop Statistics entirely for this semester
    B. Stay in the original in-person section
    C. Switch to the online asynchronous section
    D. Switch to a different chemistry course instead

### Scoring

11/11 = strong pace for 24+ on Listening · 9–10 = solid, review which
question type you missed (main idea vs. detail vs. inference/function
questions tend to need different fixes) · below 9 = check whether you're
losing points because you **missed a detail while writing notes** (fix:
write less, trust short-form notes) or because you **misunderstood why**
the speaker said something, e.g. question 4 above (fix: pay deliberate
attention to signal words like "but," "actually," "in other words," which
mark exactly where professors reveal their real point).
