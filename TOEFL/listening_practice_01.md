# TOEFL Listening — Practice Set 1 (transcript-based)

**Honest caveat first:** this file cannot play audio, and TOEFL lectures
run 3–5 minutes at natural academic pace with a single hearing — that
specific skill needs real recordings (ETS official practice sets are the
closest match). What this drill trains, which is still real exam prep:
structural note-taking under single exposure, and TOEFL's specific
question types (gist-purpose, function, inference, connecting content).
Use it alongside real audio, not instead of it.

**Protocol:** Have someone read each transcript aloud once at natural
pace (or use text-to-speech at ~150 wpm), while you take notes using
structure, not transcription — who/what's the claim/why does it
matter/how does it connect to what came before. Do not look at the
transcript text until after you've answered the questions from your
notes alone.

## Lecture — Biology (read once, ~3 minutes)

> "Let's talk about bioluminescence — organisms that produce their own
> light. It's easy to assume this is rare, but it's actually independently
> evolved more than forty separate times across different lineages, from
> fireflies to deep-sea anglerfish to certain fungi. That number alone
> tells you something: when a trait evolves independently that many
> times, it's because it solves a problem that comes up repeatedly, not
> because of one lucky accident.
>
> So what problems does it solve? In the deep ocean, where over ninety
> percent of species are estimated to be bioluminescent, the main uses are
> predation, defense, and communication. For predation, the anglerfish's
> lure is the classic case — light used as bait. For defense, some squid
> release a glowing cloud instead of ink, which, think about it, is
> actually a better decoy in total darkness than ink would be, since ink
> relies on visibility to confuse a predator and there's no light to see
> it by anyway. For communication, certain fish use light patterns
> specific to their species to find mates in total darkness, functioning
> almost like a species-specific signature.
>
> Now here's a common misconception I want to correct directly: most
> people assume bioluminescence always requires the organism to produce
> the light itself, biochemically. That's true for fireflies, where the
> organism synthesizes its own light-producing compound. But a large
> number of deep-sea species don't produce light themselves at all —
> they host colonies of bioluminescent bacteria in specialized organs,
> essentially outsourcing the chemistry, and the host organism controls
> only when the light is exposed or hidden, not how it's produced. This
> distinction matters a lot for researchers, because studying the genetics
> of 'self-luminous' species tells you nothing about the completely
> different bacterial symbiosis happening in the outsourcing species."

**Questions 1–5:**

1. What is the main purpose of the lecture?
   A. To argue that bioluminescence is evolutionarily rare
   B. To explain why bioluminescence evolved so many times and how it
      is produced
   C. To describe the life cycle of deep-sea anglerfish
   D. To compare ocean light levels at different depths
2. According to the professor, why is it significant that
   bioluminescence evolved independently more than forty times?
   A. It proves all bioluminescent species are closely related
   B. It suggests the trait solves a recurring survival problem
   C. It shows the trait is disadvantageous in most environments
   D. It means the trait is unusually difficult to study
3. Why does the professor say a glowing ink-cloud works better than
   regular ink in the deep sea?
   A. Glowing ink is more toxic to predators
   B. Regular ink requires light to be an effective decoy, which the
      deep sea lacks
   C. Regular ink dissolves too quickly in cold water
   D. Glowing ink attracts prey as well as deterring predators
4. What does the professor identify as a common misconception?
   A. That bioluminescence is useful for communication
   B. That all bioluminescent organisms produce their own light
      biochemically
   C. That anglerfish use light to attract prey
   D. That bioluminescence only occurs in the deep ocean
5. What can be inferred about researchers studying "self-luminous"
   species versus bacterial-hosting species?
   A. The two groups require entirely different lines of genetic
      research
   B. Both groups are studied using identical methods
   C. Bacterial-hosting species are more commonly studied
   D. Self-luminous species no longer interest researchers

## Conversation — Student and Academic Advisor (read once, ~2 minutes)

> **Student:** Hi, I wanted to ask about switching one of my required
> courses next semester. I'm signed up for Statistics, but I heard it
> fills up fast and has a long waitlist.
> **Advisor:** That's right, it's a popular requirement. Are you trying
> to avoid the waitlist, or is there another reason?
> **Student:** Mostly the waitlist — but also, I heard the professor
> teaching it next semester is tough, and I have a heavy course load
> already with two lab sciences.
> **Advisor:** I see. Well, there is an alternative: you could take
> Applied Data Analysis instead, which satisfies the same requirement
> and is taught by a different professor, Dr. Chen. It has smaller
> class sizes, but fair warning, it has a weekly lab component attached,
> which Statistics doesn't.
> **Student:** Hmm, an extra lab is a concern, given my schedule.
> **Advisor:** Understandable. Another option, if timing's the real
> issue: you could delay the requirement until next year and take it in
> summer term instead, when both class size and workload tend to be
> lighter, since fewer students take summer courses.
> **Student:** That actually sounds better — I wouldn't be adding
> anything to an already packed semester.
> **Advisor:** Right, and summer sections are usually capped lower, so
> you're less likely to be competing with the lab-science crowd for
> office hours and support. I'd recommend that route if your degree
> timeline allows a one-term delay.
> **Student:** I think my timeline does. I'll go with summer term, then.
> **Advisor:** I'll make a note on your file. Just make sure to register
> early even for the summer section — it's smaller, so it can still fill.

**Questions 6–8:**

6. What is the student's main concern at the start of the conversation?
   A. The cost of the Statistics course
   B. A long waitlist and a difficult professor combined with an
      already heavy course load
   C. Not being allowed to graduate on time
   D. A scheduling conflict with a lab science course
7. Why does the advisor mention Applied Data Analysis?
   A. As the only option available to the student
   B. As an alternative that satisfies the requirement but adds a lab
      component
   C. Because it has no prerequisites
   D. Because it is easier than Statistics
8. What does the student decide to do, and why?
   A. Take Applied Data Analysis, because of the smaller class size
   B. Take Statistics as originally planned, to stay on schedule
   C. Delay the requirement to summer term, to avoid adding to an
      already packed semester
   D. Drop the requirement entirely

## Answer Key

1. **B** — the lecture's throughline is "why so many independent origins,
   and how is light actually produced (self-made vs. bacterial)."
2. **B** — "when a trait evolves independently that many times, it's
   because it solves a problem that comes up repeatedly."
3. **B** — "regular ink relies on visibility to confuse a predator and
   there's no light to see it by anyway."
4. **B** — directly stated: "most people assume bioluminescence always
   requires the organism to produce the light itself... that's not [true
   for all species]."
5. **A** — "studying the genetics of 'self-luminous' species tells you
   nothing about the completely different bacterial symbiosis."
6. **B** — waitlist + tough professor + already heavy lab-science load,
   stated together as the reason.
7. **B** — advisor explicitly notes it satisfies the requirement but
   flags the added weekly lab as a tradeoff.
8. **C** — student explicitly chooses summer term specifically because
   it avoids adding to the current packed semester.

### Scoring (out of 8)

7–8 = pace for a 27+ Listening section · 5–6 = pace for 22–26 · below 5:
check whether you missed questions because your notes lacked structure
(you wrote words, not the relationship between ideas) or because you lost
focus partway through — these need different fixes. Log the result and
the specific gap in `PROGRESS_LOG.md`, and run a real ETS official
listening set in the same week.
