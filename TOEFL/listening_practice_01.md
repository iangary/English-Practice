# TOEFL Listening — Drill Set 1

**Honest limitation up front:** TOEFL Listening lectures run 3–5 minutes
at natural academic pace with a specific rhythm (thesis → examples →
digression → return to thesis) that a transcript read silently cannot
replicate — you process speech and text at different speeds and with
different memory loads. Use official ETS sample audio (the free ones on
the ETS TOEFL website) or a reputable prep platform for real listening
reps. What this file trains instead, with no audio required, is
**note-taking structure** and **function-of-what-was-said** questions,
which are where even good listeners lose points.

## Protocol

Read the transcript **once**, at normal reading speed, while taking notes
in the template below — don't write full sentences, capture structure
(who/what/why/how ideas connect). Then cover the transcript and answer
from your notes only, not from re-reading.

## Note-taking template

```
TOPIC:
MAIN POINT 1:        supporting detail:
MAIN POINT 2:        supporting detail:
MAIN POINT 3:        supporting detail:
PROFESSOR'S ATTITUDE/EMPHASIS (if any):
```

## Set 1 — Lecture (Biology)

**Transcript (read once, take notes as you go):**

> Professor: So today I want to talk about something called "mast
> seeding" — it's a pattern you see in certain trees, like oaks and
> beeches, where instead of producing a steady, moderate number of seeds
> every single year, the tree produces almost no seeds for several
> years in a row, and then suddenly produces an enormous surplus all at
> once, synchronized across an entire forest.
>
> Now, the obvious question is: why would a tree evolve to do this
> instead of just producing seeds steadily? There are two main
> explanations researchers point to. The first is what's called predator
> satiation. If a tree produced the same moderate number of seeds every
> year, seed-eating animals — squirrels, certain insects, birds — would
> adapt their populations to match that steady food supply, and
> essentially every seed produced would get eaten before it could
> germinate. But if the tree produces almost nothing for, say, three or
> four years, the predator population actually shrinks, because there
> isn't enough food to sustain it. Then, when the tree suddenly floods
> the forest with far more seeds than the now-smaller predator
> population could possibly eat, a large fraction survives simply
> because there physically aren't enough predators left to consume them
> all.
>
> The second explanation has to do with pollination efficiency — this
> applies mainly to wind-pollinated trees like oaks. A single tree
> flowering in isolation, so to speak, wastes a lot of pollen because
> there's not enough synchronized flowering nearby for cross-pollination
> to happen efficiently. But when every tree across a wide area flowers
> heavily in the same year, pollination success per flower actually goes
> up substantially, which means producing seeds in that synchronized,
> boom year is a much more efficient use of the tree's energy than
> spreading the same total effort evenly across many years.
>
> Now, here's what I find most interesting, and it's actually still a
> bit of an open question in the field: how do trees across an entire
> forest — sometimes over enormous distances — manage to synchronize
> this? The leading hypothesis is that weather acts as the shared
> trigger — say, a particularly warm, dry summer two years prior signals
> many trees simultaneously to begin the reproductive process that
> culminates in a mast year. But that doesn't fully explain cases where
> mast years are synchronized even across regions with somewhat
> different weather patterns, so there's ongoing research into whether
> trees might also be responding to some kind of chemical signaling
> between individuals, though that's considerably more speculative at
> this point.

**Questions (function + detail, as on the real test):**

1. According to the professor, what is "predator satiation"?
   A. A method trees use to poison seed predators
   B. Producing so many seeds at once that predators cannot eat them all
   C. A technique for attracting more pollinators
   D. A disease that affects seed-eating animals

2. Why does the professor mention wind-pollinated trees specifically?
   A. To contrast them with trees that don't mast seed at all
   B. To explain why synchronized flowering improves pollination efficiency
   C. To argue that wind pollination is less efficient than animal pollination
   D. To introduce a third, unrelated explanation for mast seeding

3. What is the professor's attitude toward the "chemical signaling"
   explanation for synchronization across regions?
   A. Fully convinced it is correct
   B. Dismissive — considers it disproven
   C. Cautious — notes it is speculative and still being researched
   D. Certain it will replace the weather-trigger hypothesis

4. Summarize, in your own words from your notes (not by re-reading), the
   TWO explanations given for why mast seeding evolved.

### Answer key
1. B
2. B — this is a "why does the speaker mention X" function question;
   the detail about wind pollination exists specifically to support the
   synchronized-flowering-efficiency point, not as a standalone fact.
3. C — note the explicit hedging language ("open question," "leading
   hypothesis," "considerably more speculative"). TOEFL frequently tests
   whether you can distinguish a professor's confident claims from their
   acknowledged uncertainties.
4. Predator satiation (overwhelming seed predators so enough seeds
   survive) and pollination efficiency (synchronized flowering improves
   cross-pollination success for wind-pollinated trees).

## Why this matters even without audio

Question 2 and question 3 above are both "function" and "attitude"
questions — the two types that note-takers who only write down facts
(not structure or tone) consistently miss, even when they heard every
word clearly. If your notes don't have a place to capture *why* the
professor said something or *how confident* they sounded, you will lose
these points regardless of listening ability. Fix the notes template
first; then go drill with real audio.
