# TOEFL Listening — Practice Set 1 (1 Conversation + 1 Lecture)

**Protocol:** Read each transcript below **silently, once, at normal
reading pace**, taking notes on a separate sheet exactly as you would
during the real test — capture structure (who/what/why/how points
connect), not every word. Do not reread before answering. This simulates
the real constraint: you hear it once, and good TOEFL listening scores
come from efficient note-taking, not perfect memory.

**Full section timing:** ~36 minutes for 3–4 conversations + 3–4
lectures. This set is 2 of those — cap yourself at **10 minutes total**
(read both once, answer both question sets) to keep realistic pressure.

## Conversation — Student and University Housing Office

> I've been placed in a double.
>
> **Staff:** Let me pull that up... okay, I see the application. It looks
> like singles filled up faster than usual this year — we had about 30%
> more requests than last year, and we only have a fixed number of single
> rooms in the Hartley building.
>
> **Student:** Is there anything that can be done? I specifically need
> quiet to study — I have some hearing sensitivity issues and shared
> spaces are genuinely difficult for me, not just a preference.
>
> **Staff:** Ah, okay — that actually changes things. If you have a
> documented accommodation need, that's handled through Disability
> Services, not through the general housing lottery, and requests through
> that office get priority placement regardless of when the single rooms
> filled up.
>
> **Student:** I didn't realize that was a separate process. I do have
> documentation from my doctor.
>
> **Staff:** Good — here's what I'd suggest: email Disability Services
> today with that documentation attached, cc me on it, and request an
> expedited review given that the semester starts in seventeen days. I
> can personally hold one of the two single rooms we keep in reserve for
> exactly this kind of situation, but only for about a week — after that
> I'd have to release it back into the general pool.
>
> **Student:** Okay, I'll send that email right after this. Should I still
> move into the double in the meantime?
>
> **Staff:** I'd actually recommend not unpacking fully there if you can
> avoid it — just in case the switch comes through, it's less hassle. But
> you should still check in and get your key, since you need to be
> officially housed somewhere while this is processed.

## Lecture — Introductory Economics (concept: "sunk cost fallacy")

> "I want to talk today about a pattern in decision-making called the sunk
> cost fallacy, because once you can name it, you start noticing it
> everywhere — including, probably, in your own choices this week.
>
> A sunk cost is money, time, or effort that's already been spent and
> can't be recovered, no matter what you decide next. The rational
> economic principle is that sunk costs should be irrelevant to future
> decisions — you should only weigh the costs and benefits that lie
> ahead of you, starting from right now. But in practice, people
> systematically let sunk costs influence them anyway.
>
> Here's a classic illustration. Imagine you've bought a non-refundable
> ticket to a concert, and on the day of the show, you come down with a
> bad cold and a storm is making the drive dangerous. Rationally, the
> ticket price is already spent regardless of whether you go — so the
> only question that should matter now is: would you enjoy going tonight,
> given how you feel and the driving conditions? But many people will
> force themselves to go anyway, reasoning, 'I already paid for this
> ticket,' as though attending badly, while sick, somehow recovers the
> sunk cost. It doesn't — the money's gone either way.
>
> This shows up well beyond personal spending. Businesses are a major
> source of real-world examples. There's a well-documented case from the
> aerospace industry, often called the Concorde fallacy, after the
> supersonic passenger jet: the British and French governments continued
> funding the Concorde program for years after it became clear the
> project would never be commercially profitable, in part — according to
> later analysis — because so much had already been invested that
> cancelling felt like 'wasting' that investment, even though continuing
> only added to the losses.
>
> Now, a student last term raised a good objection in office hours, which
> I think is worth addressing directly: isn't it sometimes rational to
> finish something you've started, even at a loss, if there's a chance
> conditions will improve? And the answer is: that can be rational, but
> only if the *future* expected benefit of finishing — not the past cost
> — justifies the *future* cost of continuing. If you'd evaluate
> continuing as a bad idea from a completely fresh starting point, the
> only thing making you continue anyway is the sunk cost, and that's
> exactly the fallacy.
>
> One more nuance before we move on: researchers distinguish the sunk
> cost fallacy from a related but separate behavior, loss aversion — the
> tendency to feel losses more intensely than equivalent gains. They
> often travel together in real behavior, but they're conceptually
> distinct: loss aversion is about how losses *feel*, while the sunk cost
> fallacy is specifically about letting unrecoverable past costs
> influence a forward-looking decision."

### Scoring and what to check if you missed questions

9–11/11 = solid 24+ pace · 7–8 = borderline, review note-taking
structure · below 7: check specifically whether you missed **detail**
questions (2, 4 — concrete facts) or **inference/organization** questions
(3, 9, 11 — require connecting what was implied or how ideas relate, not
just what was said). These two failure modes have different fixes:
detail misses mean take more specific notes; inference/organization
misses mean practice tracking the speaker's *purpose* in each section as
you listen, not just the content.
