# TOEFL Listening — Drill Set 01

**No audio file, so protect the exam condition that matters most: you
hear it once, take notes, and answer without re-reading the source.** Have
a TTS voice or another person read each piece aloud, once, at a natural
pace. Take notes *while* it plays — not after. Then cover the transcript
and answer the questions from your notes only.

**Note-taking target:** structure, not transcription. For the lecture,
you should come away with: the topic, 2–3 main points, and 1 specific
example per point. If your notes are full sentences, you were writing too
much and missing the next point while you wrote the last one.

**Timing:** real TOEFL gives you 3–4 lectures (3–5 min each, 6 questions)
and 2–3 conversations (3 min each, 5 questions) per section. This drill is
one of each — budget ~10 minutes including the questions.

## Conversation — Student and academic advisor (5 questions)

**Script (read aloud once, uninterrupted):**

> **Student:** Hi, I wanted to ask about switching my minor from
> Economics to Statistics. I'm a Computer Science major.
> **Advisor:** Sure, let's look at what that involves. You do realize
> you'd need two additional courses beyond what you've already taken for
> the Economics minor, right? Your Econ credits don't transfer over.
> **Student:** Right, I figured as much. I think it's worth it though —
> I'm applying to grad programs that want to see more quantitative
> coursework, and honestly, I've found I enjoy the stats courses I've
> taken so far more than the economics ones.
> **Advisor:** That's a completely reasonable reason to switch, and it's
> good that you're thinking about this now rather than senior year — you
> have time to fit the two extra courses in without delaying graduation,
> as long as you take one next semester and one the semester after.
> **Student:** Perfect, that works with my schedule. One more thing — does
> this affect my current financial aid package at all? I have a
> departmental scholarship tied to the Economics department.
> **Advisor:** That's actually a good catch, and something I can't answer
> for you — you'll need to check with the financial aid office directly,
> since scholarship terms vary. I'd do that before you file the official
> minor-change paperwork, just so you're not caught off guard.
> **Student:** Okay, I'll go there right after this. Thanks for the help.

**Questions:**

1. What is the student's main purpose for visiting the advisor?
   ____________________
2. Why does the student want to switch minors? (name both reasons given)
   ____________________
3. What constraint does the advisor mention about the Economics credits?
   ____________________
4. What does the advisor say about the student's timing in making this
   decision? ____________________
5. What should the student do before filing the official paperwork, and
   why? ____________________

<summary>Answer key</summary>

1. To ask about switching his/her minor from Economics to Statistics.
2. Grad programs want more quantitative coursework, and the student
   enjoys the stats courses more than the economics ones.
3. The Economics credits don't transfer over — two additional courses are

## Lecture — Introductory Psychology: cognitive dissonance (6 questions)

**Script (read aloud once, uninterrupted):**

> "Today I want to talk about cognitive dissonance — the mental discomfort
> people feel when they hold two contradictory beliefs, or when their
> behavior contradicts their beliefs. The classic demonstration of this
> comes from a 1959 study by Festinger and Carlsmith. Participants were
> asked to do an extremely boring task — turning pegs on a board for an
> hour — and afterward, were asked to tell the next participant, who was
> waiting outside, that the task had actually been really interesting.
> Here's the key manipulation: one group was paid 1 dollar to tell this
> lie, and another group was paid 20 dollars.
>
> Now, you'd think the people paid more would report enjoying the task
> more — after all, they got a bigger reward. But the opposite happened.
> The group paid only 1 dollar later rated the task as significantly more
> enjoyable than the group paid 20 dollars. Why? Festinger's explanation
> was that the 1-dollar group had insufficient external justification for
> lying — 1 dollar isn't enough money to explain to yourself why you'd lie
> — so to resolve the dissonance between 'I lied' and 'I'm not the kind of
> person who lies for no good reason,' they unconsciously shifted their
> actual attitude, convincing themselves the task really had been
> somewhat enjoyable. The 20-dollar group, on the other hand, had a
> perfectly sufficient external justification — 20 dollars was a lot of
> money in 1959 — so they felt no need to change their internal attitude
> at all; they could just think, 'I lied because I was paid well,' and
> feel no discomfort.
>
> This is a really counterintuitive finding, and it's the foundation for a
> huge amount of later work on attitude change — the general principle
> being that the smaller the external reward or justification for a
> behavior that conflicts with your beliefs, the more pressure there is to
> change the belief itself, rather than just tolerate the conflict."

**Questions:**

6. What is the main topic of the lecture? ____________________
7. What task were participants asked to do in the study? ____________________
8. What were the two payment conditions described? ____________________
9. Which group rated the task as more enjoyable, and was this expected or
   unexpected? ____________________
10. According to Festinger's explanation, why did the 1-dollar group
    change their attitude? ____________________
11. According to the lecture, why did the 20-dollar group *not* need to
    change their attitude?

<details>
<summary>Answer key</summary>

6. Cognitive dissonance (and the Festinger & Carlsmith 1959 study
   demonstrating it).
7. Turning pegs on a board for an hour (a boring task), then telling the
   next waiting participant that it had been interesting.
8. $1 and $20.
9. The $1 group rated it as more enjoyable — this was unexpected/the
   opposite of what you'd predict.
10. $1 wasn't sufficient external justification for lying, so to resolve
    the discomfort of having lied, they convinced themselves the task
    really had been enjoyable (changing their internal belief instead).
11. $20 was sufficient external justification on its own — they could
    attribute the lie to the payment and felt no need to change their
    actual attitude.

</details>

## Self-audit after scoring

- Look at your raw notes before you look at the answer key. Do they
  capture the *structure* (setup → manipulation → counterintuitive
  result → explanation)? If your notes are a wall of disconnected
  fragments, you were transcribing, not structuring.
- For the lecture, question 11 depends on catching a contrast ("on the
  other hand") stated once, with no repetition. If you missed it, that's
  a signal to practice flagging contrast/comparison language the moment
  you hear it, since lectures rarely restate the point.
- For the conversation, note whether you lost points on the *reason*
  questions (2, 4, 5) — TOEFL conversations are scored heavily on
  capturing *why*, not just *what*, happened.

Log your score out of 11 and whether the miss pattern is "content capture"
or "speaker's reasoning/stance" in `PROGRESS_LOG.md`.
