# TOEFL Listening — Drill Set 1 (Conversation + Lecture)

**Protocol:** Unlike reading, you get **no second pass** on real TOEFL
Listening, and no preview of questions before the audio. Simulate this
honestly:

1. Read the transcript below **once only**, straight through, at normal
   reading speed, taking notes exactly as you would during real audio
   (structure/connections, not full sentences — see `TOEFL/README.md`'s
   note on this being a common plateau point).
2. Close or cover the transcript. Answer the questions using only your
   notes — do not scroll back up to check.
3. Only after answering everything, check the answer key.

## Part 1: Conversation (student + academic advisor)

### Transcript (read once)

> **Student:** Hi, do you have a few minutes? I wanted to ask about
> switching my minor.
> **Advisor:** Sure, come in. What are you thinking of switching to?
> **Student:** Right now I'm doing a minor in Statistics, but I'm
> considering switching to Economics instead. I'm a Computer Science
> major, and honestly the stats minor felt like a safe choice when I
> declared it freshman year, but I've taken two economics electives
> since then and really enjoyed them more than I expected.
> **Advisor:** That's a reasonable thing to reconsider. How many
> credits do you already have toward the Statistics minor?
> **Student:** I've completed three of the five required courses.
> **Advisor:** And for Economics?
> **Student:** I've taken two electives, but neither one counts toward
> the core requirements — they were just electives I took out of
> interest.
> **Advisor:** Okay, so here's the thing to think about: switching now
> means starting mostly from scratch on the Economics side, since your
> two courses don't fulfill core requirements, whereas you're already
> 60% done with the Statistics minor. Given your CS major, a lot of
> advanced CS and data-focused courses actually pair very well with a
> Statistics minor — more so, honestly, than with Economics.
> **Student:** That's a fair point. I hadn't thought about how it pairs
> with my major specifically, I was mostly thinking about which classes
> I enjoyed more.
> **Advisor:** Both are valid things to weigh. My suggestion, if you're
> not in a rush: finish the Statistics minor since you're most of the
> way there, and just keep taking Economics electives for interest. You
> don't have to formally minor in something to benefit from the
> courses.
> **Student:** That actually makes a lot of sense. I think I'll stick
> with Statistics then, and just keep the Economics classes as
> electives.
> **Advisor:** Sounds like a plan. Let me know if you want help picking
> which electives to prioritize next semester.

### Questions

**1. Gist-purpose:** Why does the student visit the advisor?
(A) To complain about a required course
(B) To ask about changing her declared minor
(C) To request an extension on a deadline
(D) To ask about switching her major

**2. Detail:** How many of the required Statistics minor courses has
the student already completed?
(A) Two of five (B) Three of five (C) All five (D) None yet

**3. Detail:** Why don't the student's Economics courses count toward
an Economics minor?
(A) She got low grades in them.
(B) They were electives, not core requirement courses.
(C) She took them at a different institution.
(D) They have since been removed from the course catalog.

**4. Function/inference:** What does the advisor imply about how
Statistics pairs with the student's Computer Science major?
(A) It has no particular connection to Computer Science.
(B) It pairs better with Computer Science than Economics does.
(C) It is required for all Computer Science majors.
(D) It is being phased out as a minor option.

**5. Outcome:** What does the student decide to do by the end of the
conversation?
(A) Switch fully to an Economics minor
(B) Drop both the Statistics minor and Economics electives
(C) Finish the Statistics minor and keep taking Economics as electives
(D) Ask to double major in both fields

## Part 2: Lecture excerpt (Psychology — the bystander effect)

### Transcript (read once)

> "So we've been talking about social influence, and today's example is
> one you've probably heard referenced in the news without necessarily
> knowing the research behind it: the bystander effect. The basic
> finding is that an individual is actually *less* likely to help
> someone in an emergency when other people are present than when
> they're alone — which is counterintuitive, right? You'd think more
> people around means more help available, more likely someone steps
> in. The classic explanation researchers settled on involves two
> separate mechanisms, and it's worth keeping them distinct because
> they suggest different interventions. The first is what's called
> diffusion of responsibility — when multiple people are present, each
> individual feels less personally responsible for acting, because the
> responsibility is, in effect, shared and therefore diluted across the
> whole group. No one person feels fully on the hook. The second
> mechanism is called pluralistic ignorance — this one is subtler.
> When a situation is ambiguous, like someone collapsed on a sidewalk —
> is it a medical emergency or is this person just sleeping? — people
> look to others' reactions to decide how to interpret the situation.
> And if everyone else looks calm, each individual assumes the
> situation must not actually be an emergency, not realizing that
> everyone else is doing the exact same calculation and also staying
> calm for the same reason. So you get this feedback loop of inaction
> where everyone is actually uncertain, but everyone reads everyone
> else's outward calm as evidence that there's nothing wrong. Now, what
> actually helps counter this — and this is the practical point I want
> you to remember — training programs for bystander intervention now
> specifically teach people to single out one specific person in a
> crowd and give them a direct, specific instruction, like pointing at
> one person and saying 'you, call emergency services' rather than
> shouting a general call for help to the crowd. That directness breaks
> the diffusion of responsibility, because it assigns the task to one
> identifiable individual instead of leaving it ambiguous who should
> act."

### Questions

**6. Gist-content:** What is the main topic of this lecture excerpt?
(A) How to design psychology experiments ethically
(B) Why people are less likely to help in emergencies when others are
    present, and how training can counter this
(C) A history of emergency response training programs
(D) The difference between ambiguous and unambiguous emergencies

**7. Detail:** According to the lecture, what is "diffusion of
responsibility"?
(A) Responsibility for acting feels shared and therefore diluted among
    a group, so no individual feels fully responsible.
(B) People diffuse (spread out) physically to avoid an emergency.
(C) A form of training used to assign tasks in a crowd.
(D) The tendency to misread a collapsed person as sleeping.

**8. Detail:** According to the lecture, what is "pluralistic
ignorance"?
(A) A lack of knowledge about emergency procedures in general.
(B) People misjudging an ambiguous situation as not an emergency
    because others appear calm, not realizing others are doing the same.
(C) Ignorance of the fact that bystanders exist at all.
(D) A training deficiency among emergency responders specifically.

**9. Connecting content:** Why does the professor mention pointing at
one specific person and saying "you, call emergency services"?
(A) As an example of a mistake untrained bystanders commonly make
(B) As a practical technique that counters diffusion of responsibility
    by assigning responsibility to one identifiable person
(C) As a historical account of how the bystander effect was discovered
(D) As an example of pluralistic ignorance in action

**10. Organization:** How does the professor structure the explanation
of the bystander effect?
(A) By presenting one single cause and then its one solution
(B) By distinguishing two separate mechanisms, then describing an
    intervention that specifically targets one of them
(C) By comparing historical and modern emergency response systems
(D) By listing several unrelated case studies in chronological order
