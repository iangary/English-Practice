## Conversation — Student and academic advisor

> **Student:** Hi, I was hoping to get some advice about switching my
> minor. I'm currently doing a minor in Economics, but I've gotten really
> into the Environmental Science courses I've taken as electives.
> **Advisor:** Okay, let's look at what that would involve. How many
> Economics minor credits have you already completed?
> **Student:** Four courses — so I'm just over halfway, I think.
> **Advisor:** Right, you'd need two more for the Economics minor. For
> Environmental Science, let me check... you've taken the introductory
> course and one upper-level elective that happens to count toward it, so
> you'd actually need three more courses, not starting from zero.
> **Student:** Oh, that's better than I expected. Is there a deadline for
> declaring a minor change?
> **Advisor:** Yes — it has to be done before the add/drop deadline of
> your second-to-last semester, so you'd have until next fall, not this
> semester, which gives you some breathing room to think it over properly.
> **Student:** That's helpful. One concern — will switching affect my
> financial aid? I have a departmental scholarship tied to the Economics
> department.
> **Advisor:** That's actually an important question, and not one I can
> answer directly — scholarship terms vary a lot by donor. You'll want to
> check with the Financial Aid office specifically, not just assume it
> transfers or that it's lost, because I've seen it go either way
> depending on the exact scholarship language.
> **Student:** Okay, I'll do that before I decide anything.
> **Advisor:** Good plan. One more thing — if you do switch, I'd actually
> recommend keeping Economics as a *secondary* interest even informally,
> since the combination of environmental science and economics is
> genuinely useful for environmental policy work, which it sounds like
> might interest you given the direction of your electives.

## Lecture excerpt — Introductory Psychology (cognitive bias)

> "Today I want to talk about something called the sunk cost fallacy, and
> I want to start with an example that isn't about money, because people
> often think this only applies to finance.
>
> Imagine you're two hours into a three-hour movie, and it's genuinely
> bad — you're not enjoying it at all. The rational question at that point
> is simple: given only what happens in the remaining hour, is it worth
> staying? The two hours you've already spent are gone regardless of what
> you decide now — economists call that a sunk cost, something you can't
> get back no matter what you choose going forward. But most people don't
> reason that way. They think, 'I've already invested two hours, I might
> as well finish it' — as if leaving now would somehow retroactively waste
> the first two hours more than staying would. It wouldn't. The two hours
> are spent either way.
>
> This shows up with much higher stakes than movies, of course. Businesses
> continue funding failing projects because they've 'already invested so
> much.' Governments continue infrastructure projects well past the point
> where independent analysis suggests they should be abandoned, partly
> because canceling feels like admitting the prior spending was wasted —
> even though, again, that money is gone regardless of the decision made
> today.
>
> Now, here's the part that's genuinely interesting from a psychological
> standpoint, not just an economic one: researchers have found that the
> sunk cost fallacy is substantially reduced — though not eliminated —
> when people are explicitly asked to imagine they are a new decision-maker
> encountering the situation fresh, with no personal history of having
> made the original investment. This suggests the fallacy isn't purely
> about miscalculating costs and benefits — it's tangled up with a
> reluctance to admit, even just to ourselves, that an earlier decision we
> made was a mistake. That's an identity-protection mechanism as much as
> a math error."
