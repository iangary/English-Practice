# TOEFL Listening — Drill Set (Lecture + Conversation)

**Same caveat as always: a text file can't play audio. Use the method
below to train note-taking structure and inference, but get real TOEFL
audio (ETS's free practice test, or TPO sets) into your weekly rotation —
this drill sharpens technique, it doesn't substitute for hearing varied
accents and speech rates under time pressure.**

## Method

1. Have the passage read aloud to you once (TTS or another person), at
   normal speed, no pausing, no rereading.
2. While listening, take notes using the template below — **structure,
   not transcription.** If you're writing full sentences, you're already
   behind.
3. Cover your notes' source text. Answer the questions from your notes
   alone.
4. Reveal the transcript, check answers, and separately check: did my
   notes actually capture what I needed, or did I write the wrong things
   down even when I understood the lecture?

### Note-taking template (use this shape, not prose)

```
TOPIC: ___________________
MAIN POINT: ___________________
  → support/example 1:
  → support/example 2:
  → support/example 3:
CONTRAST/BUT: ___________________   (examiners love testing the "however")
PROF'S OPINION (if stated): ___________________
```

## Part 1 — Lecture (Biology)

**Questions 1–5.**

**1.** What is the main purpose of the lecture?
A. To describe how bioluminescence evolved in one specific species
B. To compare two competing theories of why bioluminescence evolved
C. To explain three different functions bioluminescence serves in deep-sea organisms
D. To argue that bioluminescence is less common than previously believed

**2.** According to the professor, what is surprising about the
anglerfish's use of bioluminescence?
A. It is the only known predatory use of light in deep-sea fish.
B. The light is produced by bacteria, not the fish itself.
C. It only works in very shallow water.
D. Male anglerfish do not have this ability.

**3.** Why does the professor mention the Hawaiian bobtail squid?
A. To give an example of light used for camouflage, not attraction
B. To contradict the textbook's claim about bioluminescence
C. To introduce a new topic unrelated to the main lecture
D. To describe a species that has since gone extinct

**4.** What does the professor imply about future research in this area?
A. It is unlikely to find new bioluminescent species.
B. It may be limited by how difficult deep-sea organisms are to study alive.
C. It will focus mainly on freshwater organisms instead.
D. Most of the important discoveries have already been made.

**5.** Match each function to the correct example (write the letter).

Function: ___ Predation ___ Camouflage ___ Communication

A. Hawaiian bobtail squid matching moonlight from above
B. Anglerfish lure attracting prey
C. Flashing patterns between members of the same species

<summary>Transcript (reveal only after attempting)</summary>

PROFESSOR: So today I want to talk about bioluminescence — that's the
production of light by living organisms — and specifically, why it
evolved. Now, a lot of people think of this as one thing, like it's a
single adaptation, but actually it serves at least three really distinct
functions, and I want to walk through each with an example.

First: predation. The classic example here is the anglerfish — you've
probably seen pictures, it's got that glowing lure dangling in front of
its mouth. What's actually surprising isn't that it glows — that part's
well known — it's that in most anglerfish species, the light isn't
produced by the fish's own cells at all. It comes from colonies of
bioluminescent bacteria that live symbiotically in the lure. The fish
basically farms the bacteria.

Second function: camouflage, which sounds almost contradictory — why
would producing light help you hide? But consider the Hawaiian bobtail
squid. It lives in shallow water, and from below, a predator looking up
would normally see its silhouette against the moonlit surface. So the
squid has light-producing organs on its underside that match the
intensity of the moonlight, essentially erasing its shadow. This is
called counter-illumination, and it's a completely different use of the
same basic mechanism.

Third: communication — some species flash in specific patterns to signal
to other members of their own species, for mating or territory. We know
less about this one, honestly, partly because — and this is the real
limitation in this whole field — most of these organisms live at depths
where it's extremely difficult to observe them without disturbing their
natural behavior. A lot of what we know comes from specimens brought to
the surface, which isn't the same as watching undisturbed behavior in
situ. So I'd expect future work here to be shaped as much by advances in
deep-sea observation technology as by biology itself.

**Answer key:** (1) C (2) B (3) A (4) B (5) Predation=B, Camouflage=A,
Communication=C

</details>

## Part 2 — Conversation (Campus, Student + Advisor)

**Questions 6–10.**

**6.** Why does the student visit the advisor?
A. To drop a class after the deadline
B. To ask about switching her major
C. To request an extension on a research paper
D. To complain about a professor's grading

**7.** What does the advisor say about the late drop policy?
A. It is never allowed under any circumstances.
B. It requires a documented medical or personal reason.
C. It only applies to first-year students.
D. It was eliminated this semester.

**8.** What does the student decide to do instead of dropping the class?
A. Transfer to a different section of the same class
B. Meet with the professor about a modified deadline
C. Withdraw from the university for the semester
D. Switch the class to pass/fail grading

**9.** What is the advisor's tone when discussing the pass/fail option?
A. Strongly discouraging
B. Enthusiastically supportive
C. Cautiously informative, noting a tradeoff
D. Indifferent

**10.** What does the advisor ask the student to do before their next meeting?
A. Submit a formal withdrawal form
B. Talk to the professor and report back
C. Retake a placement test
D. Pay an administrative fee

<summary>Transcript (reveal only after attempting)</summary>

STUDENT: Hi, thanks for seeing me — I'm in a bit of a bind with my
Econometrics class. I'm way behind after being sick for two weeks, and I
was hoping to drop it, but I think the deadline already passed.

ADVISOR: It did pass, about three weeks ago. That said, late drops
aren't completely off the table — they're possible, but only with
documentation, like a doctor's note covering the period you were out,
and even then it goes through a petition, which can take a couple of
weeks to be approved. It's not guaranteed.

STUDENT: Hmm. I do have a note, but I don't love the idea of waiting two
weeks not knowing.

ADVISOR: That's fair. Can I ask — how far behind are you, realistically?
Is it catchable?

STUDENT: Maybe, if I had a bit more time on the current paper that's due.

ADVISOR: Then here's another option, and honestly I'd try this first
before petitioning: talk to the professor directly about a modified
deadline for that specific paper, given the documented illness. Most
professors here are receptive to that for a documented absence, more so
than you'd expect.

STUDENT: That seems a lot simpler, actually.

ADVISOR: There's also pass/fail — you could switch the class to
pass/fail so the grade doesn't affect your GPA while you catch up. I'll
mention, though, some graduate programs do look at how many pass/fail
classes are on a transcript, so it's worth knowing that tradeoff before
you commit to it, not a reason to avoid it necessarily, just something to
weigh.

STUDENT: Okay, that's good to know. I think I'll try talking to the
professor first.

ADVISOR: Good plan. Come back and let me know how that conversation goes
— if it doesn't work out, we can look at the other options with more
time to spare.

**Answer key:** (6) A (7) B (8) B (9) C (10) B

</details>

## Scoring

9–10/10 = 27+ pace · 7–8 = 24–26 pace · below 7 = below 24 — and for
every miss, check your notes template first: if the information was
never written down, that's a note-taking fix, not a listening fix. If it
was written down correctly and you still got it wrong, that's a
question-reading fix — TOEFL Listening questions often hinge on a single
qualifying word ("mainly," "surprising," "implies") that changes which
part of your notes is relevant.
