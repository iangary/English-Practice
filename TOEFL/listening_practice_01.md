# TOEFL Listening — Practice Set 1 (text-based simulation)

**Same caveat as everywhere audio is involved:** real TOEFL Listening
plays once, with no rewind, no subtitles, and note-taking is explicitly
expected (unlike IELTS, you're allowed — encouraged — to take notes
throughout). Use text-to-speech or have someone read these aloud at a
natural pace. If reading it yourself, read it **once**, continuously,
taking notes the whole time, and do not look at the questions first —
the real test gives you no preview, which is deliberately harder than
IELTS Listening in this respect.

**Note-taking protocol:** don't try to write full sentences. Capture
structure — the lecture's main claim, 2–3 supporting points, and any
named examples/studies — not every word. If your notes are longer than
you can glance back at in 10 seconds, they're too detailed.

This set: 1 conversation + 2 lectures, matching the current (reduced)
iBT Listening format.

## Lecture 1 — Introductory Biology (keystone concept, different example from the Speaking file)

*Take notes. No preview. Transcript plays once.*

> Let's talk about trophic cascades — what happens when a change at one
> level of a food web ripples through the entire ecosystem, often in
> ways that aren't obvious at first. The textbook case is Yellowstone
> after wolves were reintroduced in 1995, having been eliminated from
> the park decades earlier. Now, you might assume the direct effect was
> simply fewer elk, since wolves prey on elk. That's true, but it's not
> the interesting part. The more significant effect was behavioral, not
> just numerical: elk began avoiding certain valley and riverside areas
> where they'd be easy for wolves to ambush, even in places wolves
> rarely actually hunted. Ecologists call this the "ecology of fear" —
> the mere presence of a predator changes prey behavior, independent of
> how many prey it actually kills. With elk avoiding riverside areas,
> willow and aspen saplings in those areas, which elk had been grazing
> down to nothing for decades, were finally able to grow past seedling
> height. Taller trees stabilized riverbanks, which changed erosion
> patterns, which in turn changed river channel shape over time — actual
> physical geography, shifted by a change in predator-prey behavior. Beaver
> populations also recovered, since they depend on the willow the elk had
> been suppressing, and beaver dams created new wetland habitat for fish
> and birds. Now, I want to flag something important about how this story
> is sometimes oversimplified in popular media: some later research has
> questioned how much of the willow recovery is actually attributable to
> wolves alone versus other factors, like changes in regional precipitation
> during the same period, or elk population management through hunting
> permits outside the park. The lesson isn't that the wolf effect wasn't
> real — multiple independent studies do support behavioral avoidance
> effects — but that attributing a complex ecosystem change to a single
> cause, even a well-documented one, usually oversimplifies several
> contributing factors operating at once.

## Lecture 2 — Art History (integrated-style reasoning, harder inference questions)

*Take notes. No preview. Transcript plays once.*

> When we talk about the Dutch Golden Age of painting in the 17th
> century, one puzzle historians have tried to explain is why this
> particular period and place produced such an enormous volume of
> paintings — far more per capita than other European regions at the
> time — and why so many of those paintings were small-scale domestic
> scenes rather than the large religious or mythological works that
> dominated elsewhere. Part of the explanation is economic, not purely
> artistic. The Dutch Republic at this time had no royal court
> commissioning large-scale religious works the way Catholic monarchies
> did, partly because it was officially Protestant, and Protestant
> reform had actually reduced demand for elaborate religious imagery in
> churches, which some reformers viewed as idolatrous. So the traditional
> patron — the Church and the royal court — largely disappeared as a
> market. But the Dutch Republic was also, at the same time, unusually
> prosperous due to trade, with a large merchant and artisan middle
> class that had disposable income but no interest in or access to
> commissioning large religious altarpieces. Painters adapted to this
> new market by producing smaller, less expensive works suited to
> private homes — portraits, still lifes, landscapes, and scenes of
> everyday domestic life — and by producing them in much greater volume,
> selling through open markets and dealers rather than through
> individual aristocratic commissions. This also explains the rise of
> specialization: rather than one painter attempting every genre, as was
> more common elsewhere, Dutch painters frequently specialized intensely
> in one narrow category — one painter known almost exclusively for
> tavern scenes, another almost exclusively for seascapes — because
> specialization let an artist produce more quickly and build a
> recognizable, marketable style in a crowded, competitive market with
> many painters chasing the same middle-class buyers. So what looks like
> a purely artistic shift in subject matter was, to a significant
> degree, downstream of a change in who was buying art and why.

### Scoring

15–17/17 (Lectures) and 5/5 (Conversation) = 28+ pace ·
Missing 2–3 overall = 24–27 pace · more than that: check whether your
misses cluster on **detail questions** (you can fix with better notes) or
**inference/"can be inferred"/organization questions** (you can fix only
by tracking the speaker's *argument structure*, not just facts, while
listening). Log which category in `PROGRESS_LOG.md` — they need different
practice, and inference questions are what separates 100+ from 110+.
