**No audio file is provided here — TOEFL Listening format is simulated as
a cold read.** Protocol: read each transcript below **exactly once, at a

## Lecture — Introductory Psychology (6 questions)

> "Today I want to talk about a phenomenon called the bystander effect —
> the finding that individuals are actually *less* likely to help someone
> in an emergency when other people are present, not more likely, which
> is the opposite of what most people would predict.
>
> This was studied intensively after a well-known 1964 case in New York
> City, where a woman was attacked while, according to initial newspaper
> reports, dozens of witnesses did nothing to intervene. Now, it's worth
> noting upfront that later investigations found those original reports
> exaggerated both the number of witnesses and their awareness of what
> was happening — so the specific case became something of a media myth.
> But the psychological phenomenon it inspired researchers to study turned
> out to be real and robust across many controlled experiments.
>
> Researchers identified two main mechanisms. The first is diffusion of
> responsibility — when multiple people are present, each individual feels
> less personal obligation to act, assuming someone else will. The second
> is what's called pluralistic ignorance — in an ambiguous situation,
> people look to others' reactions to decide how to interpret events, and
> if everyone else appears calm, each person assumes it's not actually an
> emergency, even though everyone is privately uncertain and just reading
> everyone else's outward calm.
>
> Now here's the practical implication, and this is the part worth
> remembering if you're ever in trouble yourself: because of diffusion of
> responsibility, the standard advice is to single out one specific
> person — point directly at them, make eye contact, and say something
> like 'you, in the blue jacket, call an ambulance' — rather than shouting
> for help generally. This removes the ambiguity about whose
> responsibility it is, and studies on this specific intervention show it
> substantially increases the likelihood of actually getting help."

**Questions:**

(B) Each person privately being uncertain but assuming, from others'

**5.** Why does the professor recommend pointing at a specific person and

(B) It removes ambiguity about whose responsibility it is to act,

**6. Organization:** How does the professor structure this lecture?

(D) Argues that the bystander effect does not actually exist

## Conversation — Student and academic advisor (4 questions)

> **Student:** Hi, I wanted to ask about switching my minor from
> Economics to Statistics. Is that something I can still do this late in
> my degree?
> **Advisor:** It's possible, but let's look at the numbers first. How
> many Economics credits have you completed toward the minor?
> **Student:** Three courses — that's nine credits.
> **Advisor:** And the Statistics minor requires five courses, eighteen
> credits total. Do any of your Economics courses count toward it?
> **Student:** I checked, and one of them — the econometrics course —
> overlaps and counts for both.
> **Advisor:** Okay, so that means you'd need four more Statistics
> courses, not five, since the econometrics credit transfers. That's
> doable if you take two per semester for your last two semesters,
> assuming they're offered and don't conflict with your major
> requirements.
> **Student:** That's a relief. Is there a form I need to submit?
> **Advisor:** Yes, a minor-change form, signed by both the Economics and
> Statistics department coordinators. I'd get the Statistics
> coordinator's signature first — they're sometimes slower to respond, so
> starting there avoids a bottleneck at the end.
> **Student:** Good tip. One more thing — will this delay my graduation?
> **Advisor:** Based on what you've told me, no — as long as the two
> required courses per semester don't clash with your major schedule,
> you're still on track for your original graduation date.

### Scoring
9–10/10 ≈ 28–30 section pace · 7–8 ≈ 24–27 · 5–6 ≈ 20–23 · below 5:
check whether your misses are concentrated in the lecture (structure/
organization questions — you're not tracking *how* the talk is built,
only *what* it says) or the conversation (implication questions — you're
missing the "why," not the literal content).

### Note-taking self-audit
Look at the notes you took during the single read. For the lecture, did
you capture the two named mechanisms (diffusion of responsibility,
pluralistic ignorance) as distinct items, or did you blur them into one?
That specific confusion is the single most common source of missed points
on this kind of lecture — TOEFL listening questions are often built
exactly around distinguishing two similar-sounding concepts introduced
back to back.
