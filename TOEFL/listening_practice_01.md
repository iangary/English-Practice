# TOEFL Listening — Practice Set 1 (Conversation + Lecture)

**Protocol:** Read each script aloud once at natural speed (or have
someone else read it to you) while taking notes on structure — who/what/
why/how points connect — not transcription. Then cover the script and
answer from your notes only. Real TOEFL gives you the audio once; do not
replay or re-read before answering.

A full TOEFL Listening section has multiple conversations and lectures
over ~36 minutes. This set gives you one of each (10 questions total) for
a focused drill — do a full-length section under real timing at least
once before any exam-condition week.

## Part 1: Conversation (Student + Academic Advisor)

**Context:** A student meets with his academic advisor about a scheduling
conflict.

> **Advisor:** Come in, Marcus. You wanted to talk about your course
> schedule for next semester?
> **Marcus:** Yeah, there's a problem. I need to take Organic Chemistry
> II to stay on track for the pre-med requirements, but it's only offered
> at the same time as the Statistics course I need for my minor.
> **Advisor:** I see. Let's look at the options. First — is the minor
> actually required for your degree, or is it something you added on
> your own?
> **Marcus:** It's not required, but I've already completed three of the
> four courses for it, and I really don't want that work to go to waste.
> **Advisor:** Understandable. Okay, here's one option: Statistics is
> also offered online through the extended-studies program, same
> content, same credit, different schedule — it meets in the evenings.
> **Marcus:** I didn't know that was an option. Is it taught by the same
> professor?
> **Advisor:** No, a different instructor, but the department confirmed
> the curriculum and exams are standardized across both sections, so
> there's no disadvantage either way.
> **Marcus:** That actually solves it, then. Is there anything special I
> need to do to register for the online section?
> **Advisor:** Just fill out a cross-enrollment form — I can give you one
> right now — and get it signed by the extended-studies coordinator, not
> by me, since it's a different department's system.
> **Marcus:** Great, I'll head over there after this. Thanks for figuring
> this out with me.
> **Advisor:** Of course — and for what it's worth, finishing a minor
> you're most of the way through is usually worth the extra
> administrative hassle. A lot of students abandon minors at the three-
> quarters mark and regret it later.

**Questions 1–5:**

1. Why does the student visit the advisor?
   A) To drop his minor
   B) To resolve a scheduling conflict between two required courses
   C) To ask about pre-med requirements in general
   D) To request a different professor

2. What does the advisor suggest?
   A) Dropping the Statistics course entirely
   B) Taking Organic Chemistry II in a future semester instead
   C) Taking Statistics through an online, evening section
   D) Switching his minor to a different subject

3. What does the student need to do next, according to the advisor?
   A) Email the Statistics professor
   B) Get a cross-enrollment form signed by the extended-studies
      coordinator
   C) Get the advisor's signature on a form
   D) Wait for official approval by email

4. What is the advisor's opinion about students who abandon a minor near
   completion?
   A) It's usually the right call
   B) It's something students often regret
   C) It has no real consequence either way
   D) It should be encouraged to reduce course load

5. What can be inferred about the online Statistics section?
   A) It is easier than the in-person section
   B) It has different exam content
   C) It is academically equivalent to the in-person section
   D) It does not count toward the minor

## Part 2: Lecture (Introductory Economics — Behavioral Economics)

> "Today, let's talk about what's called the 'endowment effect' — this
> comes out of behavioral economics, and it describes a pretty
> consistent finding: people tend to value something more highly simply
> because they own it, compared to an identical item they don't yet own.
> The classic demonstration of this was a study where participants were
> randomly given either a coffee mug or nothing. Those given the mug were
> then asked the lowest price they'd be willing to sell it for, and those
> without a mug were asked the highest price they'd pay to buy one.
> Classical economic theory predicts these two numbers should be roughly
> the same — ownership shouldn't change an object's value. But in
> practice, the mug owners consistently demanded a selling price roughly
> twice as high as what the non-owners were willing to pay. That gap is
> the endowment effect in action.
>
> Now, why does this matter beyond a curious lab result? It has real
> consequences in markets. Think about a homeowner pricing a house they've
> lived in for twenty years — they'll very often price it well above what
> an objective market analysis would suggest, not out of dishonesty, but
> because their own attachment to the property genuinely distorts their
> sense of its market value. Real estate agents actually train for this
> specifically, because a seller who's anchored on an inflated personal
> valuation will reject reasonable offers and let a property sit unsold
> for months.
>
> One important nuance researchers have added since the original mug
> study: the effect is substantially weaker for goods people view purely
> as 'for trade' — for example, a stockbroker trading shares doesn't
> typically show the same inflated attachment to a particular stock,
> because they never psychologically framed it as 'theirs' to begin with,
> only as inventory passing through. So the endowment effect isn't simply
> about legal ownership — it's about a *psychological* sense of
> possession, which is a distinction that matters a lot for anyone trying
> to predict when the effect will and won't show up."

**Questions 6–10:**

6. What is the main purpose of the lecture?
   A) To critique classical economic theory in general
   B) To explain the endowment effect and its real-world relevance
   C) To describe how real estate agents are trained
   D) To compare stockbrokers and homeowners

7. In the original mug study, what did classical economic theory predict?
   A) Owners would demand a much higher price than buyers would pay
   B) Owners and non-owners would value the mug about the same
   C) Non-owners would value the mug more than owners
   D) Mugs have no objective market value

8. Why does the professor mention homeowners pricing their house?
   A) To show a case where the endowment effect doesn't apply
   B) To give a real-world consequence of the effect described in the
      mug study
   C) To argue that real estate agents are usually dishonest
   D) To introduce a completely new economic concept

9. According to the lecture, why do stockbrokers show a weaker endowment
   effect for shares they trade?
   A) Shares have no real monetary value
   B) They are trained specifically to avoid the bias
   C) They don't psychologically frame the shares as personally theirs
   D) Classical economic theory does not apply to financial markets

10. What is the professor's main point in discussing the stockbroker
    example?
    A) The endowment effect depends on legal ownership, not psychology
    B) The endowment effect depends on a psychological sense of
       possession, not just legal ownership
    C) The endowment effect only applies to physical objects, not
       financial assets
    D) Real estate and stock markets operate under entirely different
       economic rules

### Scoring
9–10/10 = strong (~28–30 section score pace) · 7–8 = solid (~24–27) ·
5–6 = below target (~20–23) · Under 5: re-listen (re-read) once more,
this time pausing after every 2–3 sentences to summarize in your own
words out loud — if you can't paraphrase a chunk you just heard, you
weren't actually encoding it, just hearing it.

## Note-taking template to copy for every session

```
CONVERSATION: who / what they want / obstacle / solution offered / next step
LECTURE: main concept / example #1 (what it shows) / nuance or exception / why it matters
```

Log your raw score (X/10) and whether your miss was a **detail** miss
(missed a specific fact) or a **structure** miss (misunderstood how two
ideas connected) in `PROGRESS_LOG.md` — these two failure types need
completely different fixes.
