# TOEFL Listening — Drill Set 1

**Important limitation, read first:** TOEFL Listening is audio-only, and
this file is text. To get real value from it:

1. Load the **Transcript** into a text-to-speech reader (phone "Speak
   Screen," a browser TTS tool, or have someone read it aloud) at a
   natural, moderate pace.
2. Cover the transcript. Have only your blank note-taking area and the
   **Questions** visible.
3. Play it **once, straight through, no pausing** — exactly like the
   real test, where you cannot replay.
4. Take notes *while listening*, not after. TOEFL notes should capture
   structure (what the speaker's main claim is, and the 2–3 points/
   examples supporting it), not verbatim sentences — if you're writing
   full sentences, you're falling behind the audio.
5. Answer the questions using only your notes, then reveal the
   transcript to self-check.

## Note-taking template (copy this before you start)

```
Lecture topic:
Main claim/thesis:
Point 1:
  - detail/example:
Point 2:
  - detail/example:
Point 3 (if any):
  - detail/example:
```

## Part 1 — Lecture (Biology, ~6 minutes equivalent, 6 questions)

### Questions

1. What is the main purpose of the lecture?
   (A) To describe how bioluminescence evolved in a single ocean species
   (B) To explain why bioluminescence evolved independently multiple
       times and the main functions it serves
   (C) To argue that bioluminescence is primarily used for communication
       between species
   (D) To compare bioluminescence in land and ocean organisms

2. According to the professor, roughly how many times has
   bioluminescence evolved independently among known organisms?
   (A) Twice (B) About a dozen times (C) Over 40 times (D) Over 90 times

3. The professor mentions anglerfish as an example of which function of
   bioluminescence?
   (A) Defense through startling predators
   (B) Attracting prey
   (C) Attracting mates
   (D) Camouflage via counter-illumination

4. What is "counter-illumination," as explained in the lecture?
   (A) Producing light to blind a predator temporarily
   (B) Producing light on the underside of the body to match the
       brightness of sunlight from above, making the animal harder to
       see from below
   (C) Flashing a pattern of light to attract a mate
   (D) Absorbing light rather than reflecting it

5. Why does the professor say bioluminescence is considered strong
   evidence for convergent evolution?
   (A) Because it uses an identical gene in every species that has it
   (B) Because it only evolved in deep-sea species
   (C) Because unrelated lineages evolved the same general trait
       independently, using different biochemical mechanisms
   (D) Because it evolved only after animals moved onto land

6. What does the professor imply about why bioluminescence is so common
   specifically in the deep ocean?
   (A) There is more oxygen available at depth
   (B) In near-total darkness, even a small amount of light provides a
       significant advantage for the functions it serves
   (C) Deep-sea organisms have fewer predators overall
   (D) Deep-sea water chemically reacts to produce light naturally

### Transcript

> "Today I want to talk about bioluminescence — the production of light
> by living organisms — and specifically why it's one of the best
> examples biologists point to when discussing convergent evolution.
>
> Bioluminescence isn't one single trait that evolved once and spread.
> Current estimates suggest it has evolved independently more than
> ninety separate times across the tree of life — in bacteria, fungi,
> insects, fish, and various invertebrates — each time using somewhat
> different biochemical pathways to produce light, even though the
> general outcome looks similar. That's exactly what makes it such
> strong evidence for convergent evolution: we're not looking at one
> ancestral trait being inherited, we're looking at very different
> lineages arriving at a similar solution independently, because that
> solution happens to be useful.
>
> So what is it useful for? There are really three main functions. The
> first is attracting prey — the classic example here is the anglerfish,
> which dangles a glowing lure in front of its mouth in the pitch-black
> deep ocean, and smaller fish, drawn to the light, swim close enough to
> be caught. The second function is defense — some species, like certain
> squid, release a sudden bright flash when threatened, which can
> startle or disorient a predator for just long enough to allow an
> escape. And the third function, which is actually one of the most
> common uses in the deep sea, is camouflage, through something called
> counter-illumination. Many deep-sea fish produce a dim light on their
> undersides that roughly matches the faint sunlight filtering down from
> above — so to a predator looking upward from below, the fish blends
> into the brighter water above it instead of appearing as a dark
> silhouette.
>
> Now, why is this so overwhelmingly common specifically in the deep
> ocean, more so than in most land environments? The simplest answer is
> that in near-total darkness, even a very small, biologically
> inexpensive amount of light produces an enormous relative advantage —
> whether for luring prey, startling a predator, or disappearing into
> background light. On land, or near the surface, ambient light is
> already abundant, so producing your own light doesn't provide nearly
> the same payoff relative to its biological cost. That cost-benefit
> difference is a big part of why we see this trait evolve again and
> again specifically in deep, dark environments."

### Answer key

1. (B) — the lecture's overall purpose is both the "why multiple times"
   and "what functions" question, not just one species or one function.
2. (D) — "more than ninety separate times."
3. (B) — anglerfish lure = attracting prey.
4. (B) — matches the explicit definition given in the transcript.
5. (C) — "very different lineages arriving at a similar solution
   independently... using somewhat different biochemical pathways."
6. (B) — the cost-benefit explanation given at the end.

## Part 2 — Conversation (Student–Advisor, ~3 minutes equivalent, 5 questions)

### Questions

7. Why does the student visit the academic advisor?
   (A) To change her major
   (B) To ask about switching a required course to a different semester
   (C) To appeal a grade she received
   (D) To request an overload of credits

8. What is the advisor's initial concern about the student's request?
   (A) The course isn't offered in the semester she wants
   (B) She hasn't completed a prerequisite yet
   (C) She has already missed the registration deadline
   (D) The course is already full

9. What solution does the advisor propose?
   (A) She should take the prerequisite over the summer
   (B) She should request a waiver from the department
   (C) She should take the course anyway and catch up independently
   (D) She should drop the course entirely

10. What is the student's reaction to this proposal?
    (A) She immediately agrees
    (B) She is concerned about the extra workload
    (C) She rejects it outright
    (D) She asks to speak to a different advisor

11. What does the advisor say he will do next?
    (A) Email the department to confirm the waiver process
    (B) Register her for the course immediately
    (C) Cancel her current schedule
    (D) Refer her to financial aid

### Transcript

> **Student:** Hi, thanks for seeing me. I wanted to ask about switching
> my statistics course — I'm currently signed up for next fall, but I'd
> really like to take it this spring instead, if that's possible.
>
> **Advisor:** Let me pull up your record... okay, I see the course. The
> one issue is that this course technically requires Introduction to
> Probability as a prerequisite, and according to your transcript,
> you're scheduled to take that same prerequisite this coming fall — so
> if you moved statistics to the spring, you'd actually be taking it
> before the prerequisite, not after.
>
> **Student:** Oh, I didn't realize that was an issue. Is there any way
> around it?
>
> **Advisor:** There's one option: the department does sometimes allow a
> prerequisite waiver if a student can demonstrate equivalent background
> — for example, if you'd already covered similar material in another
> course, or could pass a short placement check. Given your math
> background, I think you'd have a reasonable case, but you'd need to
> get the waiver approved before spring registration closes.
>
> **Student:** That sounds doable, but I have to admit I'm a little
> worried about jumping into statistics without having formally taken
> the probability course first — will I be behind the other students?
>
> **Advisor:** It's a fair concern, but the overlap isn't total — the
> first two or three weeks of statistics do review the probability
> basics you'd need, so with a bit of extra reading beforehand, most
> students in your position manage fine. It's really your call, though.
>
> **Student:** Okay, I think I'd like to try for the waiver, then.
>
> **Advisor:** Great — I'll email the department today to confirm exactly
> what the waiver process requires, and I'll forward that to you as soon
> as I hear back.

### Answer key

7. (B)
8. (B)
9. (B)
10. (B)
11. (A)

### Scoring

11/11 correct ≈ solid 26–30 Listening pace · 9–10 ≈ low-to-mid 20s ·
7–8 ≈ high teens to low 20s. Below 7: check your notes against the
transcript line by line — if your notes were accurate but you still
answered wrong, it's an inference problem (practice connecting detail to
"what is the advisor's concern," not just recalling the detail itself);
if your notes were missing the key line entirely, it's a note-taking
problem (you're writing too much verbatim and falling behind). Log which
one in `PROGRESS_LOG.md`.
