# TOEFL Speaking — Drill Set

**Protocol:** Record every response. TOEFL Speaking is scored heavily on
**delivery** (pacing, clarity) and **content development** (did you
actually complete the task with specific detail) — grammar accuracy
matters less here than on Writing, so stop over-correcting mid-sentence
and just keep talking; restarts cost you more than a small grammar slip.

## Task 1: Independent (15s prep, 45s speak)

Pick one, set the timer, speak the full 45 seconds — running out of
content at 25 seconds is the most common score suppressor on this task.

- Some people prefer to work for a large company; others prefer a small
  company. Which do you prefer and why?
- Describe a teacher who had a significant positive influence on you.
- Would you rather take a class that requires a lot of writing or one
  that requires a lot of group discussion? Explain why.

## Task 2: Integrated — Campus situation (reading + "listening") (45s prep, 60s speak)

**Reading (45 seconds):**

> **Notice from University Housing:** Starting next semester, the
> university will no longer guarantee overnight guest parking in
> residence hall lots. Students wishing to host overnight guests with
> cars must now request a temporary permit at least 48 hours in advance,
> subject to availability. The university cites a significant increase in
> parking complaints from residents unable to find spots near their own
> buildings.

**Listening (simulated conversation, read once):**

> **Student 1:** "Honestly, I think this is going to cause more problems
> than it solves. Most of us don't know 48 hours ahead of time when a
> friend or family member is going to visit — sometimes people just show
> up, especially for emergencies."
> **Student 2:** "Yeah, and the 'subject to availability' part is vague.
> What happens if I request it on time and there's still no spot? Do I
> just tell my visiting parent to park somewhere illegal?"

**Your task:** Summarize the student's opinion about the university's
announcement and the reasons he/she gives for holding that opinion.

## Task 3: Integrated — Academic (50s prep, 60s speak)

**Reading (45 seconds):**

> **Confirmation bias** is the tendency to search for, interpret, and
> recall information in a way that confirms one's existing beliefs, while
> giving disproportionately less attention to information that
> contradicts them. This tendency is thought to operate largely outside
> conscious awareness and can affect judgment even among experts trained
> to be objective.

**Listening (simulated lecture excerpt, read once):**

> "So here's a classic example from a study on jury decision-making.
> Researchers gave mock jurors ambiguous evidence in a case, but first
> told half of them the defendant had a prior record and the other half
> that the defendant had no record. Jurors who'd been told about the
> prior record interpreted the exact same ambiguous evidence as more
> incriminating, and reported after the fact that they felt their
> conclusion followed straightforwardly from the evidence — they didn't
> perceive themselves as having been biased at all. That last part is
> what makes confirmation bias so hard to correct: the people experiencing
> it feel like they reasoned their way to the answer neutrally."

**Your task:** Explain the concept of confirmation bias using the
specific example from the lecture.

## Task 4: Integrated — Academic, listening only (20s prep, 60s speak)

**Listening (simulated lecture excerpt, read once):**

> "Keystone species are organisms that have a disproportionately large
> effect on their ecosystem relative to their population size. Take sea
> otters along the Pacific coast. Otters prey on sea urchins, which in
> turn graze on kelp. When otter populations were decimated by historical
> hunting, urchin populations exploded unchecked, and they devastated the
> kelp forests, turning previously rich underwater habitats into what
> ecologists call 'urchin barrens' — areas nearly devoid of the other
> species that depended on kelp for food and shelter. When otter
> populations later recovered in some areas, urchin numbers fell back
> under control, and kelp forests — along with the fish and invertebrates
> they supported — recovered too."

**Your task:** Using the example of sea otters, explain what a keystone
species is and why it matters to an ecosystem.

## Self-audit after each recording

- Did you organize your answer with a clear structure (main point first,
  then supporting detail), or did you ramble toward a conclusion?
- Did you fill the full time allotment with relevant content, or trail
  off early?
- For Tasks 2–4: did you accurately represent both the reading/listening
  content, or did you substitute your own opinion for what the source
  material actually said? (These tasks are scored on accurate synthesis,
  not personal opinion.)
- Pace: aim for clear, natural speed — rushing to fit more in usually
  hurts clarity more than it helps content score.

Log a self-estimated 0–4 score per task (TOEFL Speaking's internal
rubric scale) and your biggest gap in `PROGRESS_LOG.md`.

## Set 2 — Harder Content, Same Clock

The timing stays identical to Set 1 (don't shortcut your own prep time —
that's not what makes this harder). What's harder is the density of the
source material: these readings and lectures pack more ideas per second,
so you have less slack to "wait for the main point" before you start
taking notes.

### Task 1: Independent (15s prep, 45s speak)

- Some people believe it's better to make decisions quickly; others
  believe it's better to take time and think carefully before deciding.
  Which do you think is better, and why?
- Describe a time you changed your opinion about something important
  after encountering new evidence. What changed your mind?

### Task 2: Integrated — Campus situation (45s prep, 60s speak)

**Reading (45 seconds):**

> **Notice from the Registrar's Office:** Beginning next semester,
> priority course registration will be determined primarily by number of
> credits completed rather than by declared major. The university states
> this change will give students closer to graduation a fairer chance at
> required courses that fill up quickly each term.

**Listening (simulated conversation, read once):**

> **Student 1:** "I get the logic, but this really hurts transfer
> students. I've got way fewer credits here even though I'm only a year
> from graduating, so under this system I'd register *after* a
> first-year student with more transferred credits."
> **Student 2:** "Right, and it assumes credit count tracks how close you
> actually are to finishing your specific major, which isn't always
> true — some majors just require more credits than others to begin
> with."

**Your task:** Summarize the student's opinion of the policy change and
the reasons given for that opinion.

### Task 3: Integrated — Academic (50s prep, 60s speak)

**Reading (45 seconds):**

> The **sunk cost fallacy** describes the tendency to continue investing
> in a decision based on the resources already spent on it, rather than
> on a fresh evaluation of its future costs and benefits. Economically,
> resources already spent are "sunk" — unrecoverable regardless of what
> is decided next — and so, in theory, should not factor into a rational
> decision going forward.

**Listening (simulated lecture excerpt, read once):**

> "Here's a case that comes up constantly in business. A company spends
> three years and considerable money developing a new product line.
> Partway through, market research starts showing the product won't be
> profitable even if finished — a competitor has already captured the
> market it was aimed at. But executives, pointing to the three years and
> the budget already committed, push to finish it anyway rather than
> write off the investment and redirect those remaining resources
> somewhere with better prospects. The product launches, underperforms
> exactly as the research predicted, and the company loses even more than
> it would have by stopping early — the original investment was already
> gone either way; continuing just added to the loss instead of limiting
> it."

**Your task:** Explain the concept of the sunk cost fallacy using the
specific example from the lecture.

### Task 4: Integrated — Academic, listening only (20s prep, 60s speak)

**Listening (simulated lecture excerpt, read once):**

> "Economists talk about diminishing marginal utility — the idea that
> each additional unit of something provides less added satisfaction than
> the one before it. Picture someone who hasn't eaten all day being given
> slices of pizza one at a time. The first slice is intensely satisfying —
> they were genuinely hungry. The second is still good, but noticeably
> less thrilling than the first. By the fifth or sixth slice, they're not
> just failing to enjoy it more, they may actively feel worse eating it.
> The *total* satisfaction keeps growing with each slice for a while, but
> the *additional* satisfaction each new slice provides keeps shrinking,
> and can even go negative. This is why, economically, giving someone
> twice as much of something essentially never makes them twice as
> happy."

**Your task:** Using the pizza example, explain the concept of
diminishing marginal utility.

### Extra self-audit for this set only

Because the source material is denser, check specifically: did you
report the *actual mechanism* given in the example (the specific cause
leading to the specific effect), or did you summarize it at a level so
general it would apply to almost any example of the concept? Vague
restatement is the single biggest score suppressor once the content
itself gets harder — specificity is what the rubric is actually
rewarding here.
