# TOEFL Integrated Writing — Set 2 (Harder: subtler lecture-reading mismatch)

**What makes this set harder than `writing_tasks.md` Set 1:** Set 1's
lecture directly contradicts each reading point in an obvious 1-to-1
way. On the real TOEFL, and in this set, the lecture sometimes
**reframes** or **narrows** a point rather than flatly denying it — if
you only listen for "the opposite," you'll miss the actual relationship
and lose Development/Support points even with a well-organized essay.

**Timing is unchanged: 3 min reading, read the lecture once, 20 min to
write, 150–225 words.** The difficulty is in the content, not the clock.

## Reading passage (3 minutes)

Urban planners have increasingly promoted "15-minute cities" — urban
designs in which residents can reach daily necessities (groceries,
schools, healthcare, work) within a 15-minute walk or bike ride from
home. Proponents argue this model offers three major benefits. First, it
reduces car dependency, lowering both traffic congestion and carbon
emissions, since residents no longer need to drive long distances for
routine errands. Second, it strengthens local economies, because
concentrating daily needs within neighborhoods increases foot traffic
for small, local businesses rather than large retailers located far from
residential areas. Third, it improves public health outcomes, as
walking and cycling for daily tasks naturally increases residents'
physical activity without requiring deliberate exercise programs.

## Lecture transcript (listen once — read only once, then cover it)

"The 15-minute city concept sounds appealing on paper, but let's look
more carefully at what actually happens when cities try to implement it.
On traffic: several European pilot programs have found that while
short local trips do decrease, total driving doesn't necessarily fall,
because residents still need to travel outside their neighborhood for
specialized work, family visits, or services that can't realistically
exist in every quarter of a city — so the emissions benefit is often
much smaller than projected, concentrated mainly in trip types that were
a small share of total driving to begin with.

"On local economies: there's actually decent evidence for the local
business argument in city centers with existing walkable infrastructure.
The real problem shows up in car-dependent suburbs retrofitted toward
this model — without the population density to support a full range of
businesses, you often just get a few convenience stores and coffee
shops, not the complete 'daily necessities' promised, while rents near
the newly desirable walkable core rise and push out exactly the
lower-income residents such models are often pitched as helping most.

"And on public health: yes, walking and cycling increase, but researchers
have also found that concentrating amenities within small zones can
increase local air pollution exposure, specifically for residents near
the now-busier local streets and delivery routes, partially offsetting
the exercise benefit for exactly the people living closest to the
busiest 15-minute hubs."

## Your task

Summarize the points made in the lecture, explaining specifically how
they relate to (not simply "disagree with") points made in the reading
passage.

## Self-check before you grade yourself

Before scoring, verify you captured the actual relationship for each
point — this is the part this set is specifically drilling:

- **Traffic:** Did you write that the lecture simply "disagrees," or did
  you capture that it concedes short trips decrease but argues the
  *overall* emissions benefit is overstated because of trip types the
  model doesn't address? The second is the accurate relationship.
- **Local economies:** Did you notice the lecture **partially agrees**
  (city centers) while complicating the claim for a specific context
  (retrofitted suburbs)? An essay that frames this as flat
  "contradiction" has misread the lecture and will lose Development
  points even with perfect grammar.
- **Public health:** Did you capture the *offsetting* relationship
  (exercise gain partially cancelled by pollution exposure for a
  specific subgroup) rather than a simple "the lecture says exercise
  doesn't help"?

## Scoring note

A TOEFL Integrated Writing essay that correctly identifies "partial
agreement, reframed in a specific context" scores higher on Development
than one that forces every point into a clean contradiction, even if the
forced version reads more smoothly. Precision of relationship beats
smoothness of prose on this task specifically — the opposite priority
from Independent Writing.
