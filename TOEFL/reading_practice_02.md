## The Dating of Antarctic Ice Cores

**[1]** Antarctic ice cores are widely regarded as among the most
reliable archives of Earth's past atmosphere, preserving tiny bubbles of
ancient air trapped as snow compacted into ice over hundreds of
thousands of years. Researchers extract these cores by drilling
kilometers into the ice sheet, then analyze the trapped air for carbon
dioxide and methane concentrations, alongside isotopic measurements of
the ice itself that serve as a proxy for past temperature. The oldest
continuous Antarctic core currently recovered extends back roughly
800,000 years, and a long-sought "oldest ice" project has targeted
sites believed to preserve ice over a million years old, though drilling
at such sites is technically formidable given the extreme depths and the
risk that older ice near bedrock has been deformed or mixed by the
immense pressure of the ice sheet above it.

**[2]** Before any climate data drawn from a core can be trusted,
however, researchers must solve a prior problem: establishing an
accurate age scale, assigning a reliable date to each depth within the
core. This is considerably more complicated than it might first appear,
because a critical complication arises from the fact that air bubbles
are not sealed at the moment snow falls. Snow accumulates as a porous
layer, firn, through which air can still circulate and gradually mix with
the atmosphere above; only once firn compacts further into solid ice,
a process that can take decades to several thousand years depending on
accumulation rate and temperature, do the air bubbles finally seal. This
means that at any given depth, the surrounding ice is measurably older
than the air trapped within it — an offset researchers must calculate
separately, and one that can span centuries at slow-accumulation sites
such as those favored for "oldest ice" drilling.

**[3]** Dating methods vary by depth and by how much independent evidence
exists for each segment of a core. In the shallowest sections, scientists
can count annual layers directly, in much the same manner as counting
tree rings, since seasonal variation in snow chemistry and dust content
leaves a visible layer for each year of accumulation. This approach
becomes impractical at greater depths, however, because accumulated
pressure gradually thins and eventually obscures individual annual
layers, forcing researchers to rely instead on modeling ice flow and
compaction rates, calibrated against reference points such as volcanic
ash layers from eruptions whose dates are independently known from other
sources, including tree-ring and marine sediment records.

**[4]** A further, and more recently developed, dating method exploits
the fact that certain variations in Earth's orbit and axial tilt alter
the intensity of incoming solar radiation in highly predictable,
mathematically calculable cycles stretching back millions of years.
Because these orbital cycles are understood to drive long-term shifts in
climate, and because ice cores record climate signals that should align
with predicted shifts, researchers can tune an otherwise uncertain ice
core age scale by matching its climate signal to the independently
calculated orbital cycle, a technique that has become especially
valuable for dating the deepest, most compacted sections of a core where
direct layer counting is impossible. Critics of this technique caution
that it risks a degree of circularity, since it assumes in advance that
ice core climate signals must align closely with orbital forcing, which
could in principle obscure genuine discrepancies that would otherwise
reveal gaps or disturbances in a given core.

**[5]** These dating challenges carry consequences well beyond
technical bookkeeping. A core's entire scientific value rests on the
accuracy of its age scale: a core showing that carbon dioxide levels
rose sharply at a particular point in the past means little for
understanding climate mechanisms if the attached date is wrong by
thousands of years, since the surrounding chronology of other climate
events against which it would be compared would be misaligned as well.
For this reason, when a new Antarctic core is drilled, establishing and
cross-checking its age scale against independent methods typically
consumes as much research effort as the climate measurements that
ultimately make the project famous.
