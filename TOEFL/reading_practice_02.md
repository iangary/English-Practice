## Coral Bleaching and the Limits of Symbiosis

**[1]** Reef-building corals owe their vivid color, and much of their
energy budget, to a mutualistic relationship with microscopic algae
called zooxanthellae, which live within the coral's tissue. The algae
photosynthesize and pass a substantial share of the resulting sugars to
their coral host, while the coral provides the algae a protected,
sunlit habitat and a steady supply of the carbon dioxide and nutrients
photosynthesis requires. Under stable conditions, this exchange is so
efficient that reef-building corals can thrive in the nutrient-poor
waters of tropical oceans, environments that would otherwise support
little complex marine life.

**[2]** This partnership, however, carries a vulnerability that has
become increasingly consequential: when water temperatures rise even
slightly above a coral species' tolerance range for a sustained period,
the photosynthetic machinery of the zooxanthellae becomes damaged,
producing reactive molecules that are toxic to the coral tissue housing
them. In response, the coral expels its algae en masse, a process
termed "bleaching" for the stark white appearance of the coral's
calcium carbonate skeleton showing through its now-transparent tissue.
A bleached coral is not immediately dead; without its primary energy
source, however, it survives on reserves and can only persist for a
limited window before starving, unless water temperatures drop and
zooxanthellae are able to recolonize its tissue.

**[3]** For decades, bleaching was understood primarily as a binary
stress response: temperatures either stayed within tolerable range, or
they did not. More recent research has complicated this picture by
showing that coral colonies host not one but several genetically
distinct lineages of zooxanthellae, which differ considerably in heat
tolerance. Some coral species appear able to shift the proportion of
heat-tolerant algal lineages within their tissue following a moderate,
non-lethal heat stress event, effectively "pre-adapting" to a warmer
baseline before a more severe event occurs. This finding has prompted
some researchers to propose assisted interventions, such as
deliberately inoculating vulnerable reefs with heat-tolerant algal
strains sourced from naturally heat-resistant reef systems elsewhere.

**[4]** Such interventions remain controversial. Critics point out that
the long-term fitness consequences of a coral hosting a non-native
algal lineage are not well understood; some evidence suggests that
heat-tolerant lineages, while better at surviving thermal stress,
provide their coral hosts with measurably less energy under normal
conditions than the lineages they replace, creating a possible trade-off
between short-term survival and long-term growth and reproduction.
Proponents counter that given the pace of ocean warming, a coral reef
that survives in a diminished state is preferable to one that does not
survive at all, and that the trade-off, while real, is not yet well-
enough quantified to justify inaction.

**[5]** Underlying this debate is a broader uncertainty about the pace
at which coral populations can adapt relative to the pace of warming
itself. Natural selection favoring heat-tolerant coral genotypes does
appear to be occurring in some reef systems already repeatedly exposed
to thermal stress, suggesting that evolutionary rescue is not purely
hypothetical. Whether this process can keep pace with projected warming
over the coming decades, however, remains one of the central open
questions in reef conservation biology — and the answer will likely
differ considerably between reef systems, depending on factors such as
existing genetic diversity, the frequency of past thermal stress
events, and local oceanographic conditions that affect how quickly heat
dissipates.
