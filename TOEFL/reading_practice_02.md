## Coral Bleaching and the Limits of Adaptation

**[1]** Coral reefs derive both their color and most of their energy from
zooxanthellae, microscopic algae that live symbiotically within coral
tissue, photosynthesizing and sharing the resulting nutrients with their
host in exchange for shelter and compounds the algae need to
photosynthesize. When ocean temperatures rise even 1–2°C above the local
summer maximum for a sustained period, this relationship breaks down: the
algae's photosynthetic machinery becomes damaged, producing reactive
molecules that are toxic to the coral, and the coral responds by expelling
its algae en masse. Without zooxanthellae, the coral's tissue becomes
nearly transparent, revealing the white calcium carbonate skeleton
beneath—the phenomenon known as coral bleaching. Bleached coral is not
dead, but it is starving, and if normal temperatures do not return within
weeks, the coral will die from energy deprivation or increased
susceptibility to disease.

**[2]** For decades, researchers assumed that reefs exposed to repeated
thermal stress would simply decline over time, each bleaching event
weakening the reef further. Field observations over the past twenty years
have complicated this picture. Some reef systems subjected to repeated
heat stress show evidence of acquired thermal tolerance: corals that
survive one bleaching event sometimes withstand a subsequent, equally
severe thermal event with less mortality than naive corals of the same
species experiencing heat stress for the first time. Several mechanisms
have been proposed, including the possibility that surviving corals host
different, more heat-tolerant strains of zooxanthellae than they did
before bleaching, effectively swapping algal partners for one better
suited to warmer water.

**[3]** This phenomenon, sometimes called the "adaptive bleaching
hypothesis," has been invoked—somewhat prematurely, according to many
coral biologists—as grounds for optimism about reefs' prospects under
continued warming. Critics point out that acquired tolerance observed in
controlled studies and in a subset of reef systems does not establish that
coral adaptation can keep pace with the rate of ocean warming projected
over the coming decades. Thermal tolerance gains documented so far are
typically on the order of a degree Celsius or less, while climate models
project warming of several degrees by the end of the century under
current emissions trajectories. Moreover, the mechanism that confers
tolerance in one coral species does not necessarily transfer to others;
reef systems are composed of dozens or hundreds of coral species, many of
which have shown little or no evidence of comparable tolerance gains.

**[4]** A further complication is that acquired tolerance, where it does
occur, often carries a measurable cost. Corals hosting heat-tolerant algal
strains frequently exhibit slower growth rates and reduced reproductive
output compared to corals hosting their original, heat-sensitive
partners under normal conditions. This creates a trade-off: a reef that
survives marine heatwaves by hosting tougher algae may do so at the cost
of the growth and reproduction needed to keep the reef structurally
intact and capable of recovering its full biodiversity between stress
events. Some researchers describe this as trading short-term survival for
long-term resilience, a trade whose net effect on reef ecosystems over
multiple decades is not yet well understood.

**[5]** The practical stakes of resolving this uncertainty are
considerable. Conservation strategies increasingly include "assisted
evolution" programs that selectively breed corals for heat tolerance or
actively introduce heat-tolerant algal strains into reef systems, in
effect attempting to accelerate a process that might otherwise take many
coral generations to occur naturally, if it occurs at all. Proponents
argue that, given the pace of warming, intervention is justified even
without complete certainty about long-term trade-offs. Skeptics counter
that introducing engineered or selectively bred coral stocks into wild
reef systems at scale is itself a significant, difficult-to-reverse
intervention, and that resources might be better directed toward reducing
the thermal stress itself—namely, emissions reductions and protection of
reefs from other compounding stressors such as pollution and
overfishing—rather than engineering around a problem whose root cause
lies elsewhere.

## Questions

**1. Vocabulary in context:** The word "naive" in paragraph 2 is closest

(A) inexperienced in this specific context (B) genetically identical
(C) younger in age (D) located in a different region

**2.** According to paragraph 1, coral bleaching occurs when
(A) the coral skeleton dissolves in warmer water
(B) corals expel the algae living within their tissue
(C) algae consume the coral's calcium carbonate
(D) coral disease directly kills the zooxanthellae

**3.** The word "this picture" in paragraph 2 refers to
(A) the assumption that reefs would simply decline with repeated stress
(B) the process by which zooxanthellae photosynthesize
(C) the discovery of coral bleaching in the nineteenth century
(D) the method used to measure ocean temperature

**4. Inference:** It can be inferred from paragraph 3 that most coral
biologists view the "adaptive bleaching hypothesis" as
(A) completely disproven by subsequent research
(B) a real but limited phenomenon, insufficient on its own to offset
    projected warming
(C) applicable equally to all coral species studied so far
(D) more significant than the threat posed by rising temperatures

**5.** According to paragraph 4, hosting heat-tolerant algal strains is
associated with which trade-off for coral?
(A) Increased vulnerability to disease but faster reproduction
(B) Slower growth and reduced reproduction compared to normal conditions
(C) Complete loss of photosynthetic capacity
(D) Immediate death within one reproductive cycle

**6.** Which of the following best describes the disagreement presented
in paragraph 5?
(A) Whether coral reefs are worth protecting at all
(B) Whether to prioritize active genetic/ecological intervention or
    addressing the root causes of thermal stress
(C) Whether zooxanthellae are harmful or beneficial to coral
(D) Whether bleaching events have increased in frequency

**8.** Why does the author mention "assisted evolution" programs in
paragraph 5?
(A) To present a real-world application of the adaptive tolerance

**9. Rhetorical purpose:** Why does the author include the detail that
tolerance gains are "typically on the order of a degree Celsius or less"

    projected warming of several degrees

**10. Prose Summary:** An introductory sentence is provided: *"Coral
bleaching research has revealed both an unexpected capacity for
adaptation and significant limits on that capacity."* Select the
**THREE** answer choices that express the most important ideas in the
passage.

(A) Coral bleaching occurs when stressed coral expels its symbiotic

(B) Some corals show acquired thermal tolerance after surviving a
    bleaching event, sometimes by hosting different algal strains.
(C) Zooxanthellae are entirely unrelated to coral coloration.
