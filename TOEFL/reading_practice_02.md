## The Coral Bleaching Paradox

**[1]** Coral reefs derive both their vivid color and the majority of
their energy from a symbiotic relationship with microscopic algae called
zooxanthellae, which live within coral tissue and supply the coral with
nutrients through photosynthesis in exchange for shelter and access to
sunlight. When corals experience prolonged thermal stress, they expel
these algae in a process known as bleaching, leaving the coral skeleton
visible through transparent tissue and depriving the coral of its primary
food source. Bleaching itself does not necessarily kill coral outright;
corals can survive for weeks or even months without their symbionts and
may recover if temperatures return to normal quickly enough, but
prolonged bleaching events dramatically increase mortality.

**[2]** For years, researchers treated bleaching as a uniformly
destructive event to be prevented at all costs. More recent research has
complicated this picture by revealing that not all coral-algae
pairings are equally heat-tolerant, and that bleaching, while harmful in
the short term, can sometimes result in a coral re-acquiring a different,
more heat-resistant strain of zooxanthellae after the event. This
phenomenon, termed the "adaptive bleaching hypothesis," suggests that
bleaching may function, in some circumstances, as a mechanism that allows
corals to shed a poorly matched symbiont and acquire one better suited to
warmer conditions—a process that would otherwise be nearly impossible
given how deeply integrated the original symbiont is in healthy tissue.

**[3]** Evidence for this hypothesis remains mixed. Some field studies
following the severe 1997–1998 El Niño bleaching event found that
surviving coral colonies in certain reef systems did host different, more
thermally tolerant algal strains afterward compared to before the event,
consistent with adaptive shuffling. However, critics point out that this
pattern may simply reflect selective mortality rather than active
adaptation: colonies that happened to already host more tolerant strains
survived, while others died, producing a population-level shift in
average tolerance without any individual coral actually having swapped
its symbionts. Distinguishing these two explanations requires tracking
individual coral colonies over time rather than comparing reef-wide
averages before and after an event, a far more resource-intensive
research design that only a handful of long-term monitoring programs
have been able to sustain.

**[4]** Even where genuine symbiont shuffling does occur, researchers
caution against treating it as a reliable rescue mechanism for reefs
facing climate change. Heat-tolerant algal strains, while better able to
withstand high temperatures, frequently provide their coral hosts with
measurably less energy under normal conditions than the less
tolerant strains they replace, a trade-off that can leave corals more
heat-resistant but also slower-growing and less able to compete for
space or recover from other stressors such as disease or physical
damage. This trade-off implies that even successful adaptive shuffling
may not be the unambiguous good news it was initially framed as, but
rather a survival strategy that trades one vulnerability for another.

**[5]** The frequency of bleaching events further limits how much
adaptive shuffling can realistically help. Under historical climate
conditions, reefs typically experienced severe bleaching events decades
apart, providing ample time for coral growth, reproduction, and reef
structure to recover between episodes. Current projections suggest that
by mid-century, many reef systems will experience severe bleaching
conditions annually or near-annually, compressing the recovery window to
a fraction of its historical length. Under such compressed timelines,
even reefs capable of adaptive shuffling may not have sufficient time
between events to rebuild the structural complexity that supports the
broader reef ecosystem, suggesting that symbiont flexibility alone is
unlikely to offset the pace of projected warming.

**5.** According to paragraph 4, what trade-off is associated with

(D) They eliminate the need for coral to photosynthesize at all.
