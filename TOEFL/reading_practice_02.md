## Octopus Cognition and the Limits of Comparative Intelligence

**[1]** Among invertebrates, the octopus presents an unusual puzzle for
researchers studying the evolution of intelligence. Octopuses solve
problems that would challenge many vertebrates—unscrewing jar lids,
navigating mazes, and in captivity, reportedly recognizing individual
human caretakers and directing jets of water at those they appear to
dislike—despite having a nervous system architecture radically different
from that of mammals or birds, the groups in which complex cognition has
traditionally been studied. Roughly two-thirds of an octopus's
approximately 500 million neurons are located not in a centralized brain
but distributed across its eight arms, each of which can process sensory
information and execute coordinated movements with a striking degree of
autonomy, even when severed from the central brain under laboratory
conditions.

**[2]** This decentralized architecture has led some researchers to
question whether concepts of intelligence developed primarily through
studying centralized, mammalian-style brains are even applicable to
octopus cognition, or whether doing so risks what one prominent
cephalopod researcher has termed "neural chauvinism"—evaluating a
fundamentally alien cognitive system by criteria implicitly calibrated to
resemble our own. An octopus arm exploring a crevice for food appears to
make local decisions—whether to continue probing, retract, or grip—with
only general directives, rather than precise motor commands, arriving
from the central brain, a division of labor that has no clear analog in
vertebrate nervous systems and complicates any straightforward comparison
of "how smart" an octopus is relative to, say, a crow or a dog.

**[3]** Laboratory studies have nonetheless documented capacities that
appear genuinely cognitive rather than merely reflexive. In one
widely cited series of experiments, octopuses learned to navigate
transparent mazes to reach food, and, notably, retained the learned
route across repeated trials separated by intervals long enough to rule
out simple sensory tracking of a scent trail. In another study,
octopuses presented with a transparent container holding food, sealed
with a screw-on lid, were able to learn to open the container through
trial and error, and some individuals subsequently opened similar
containers markedly faster on repeat trials—a result interpreted as
evidence of learning and memory retention, though critics note that an
alternative explanation involving refined motor skill rather than
conceptual understanding of the lid mechanism cannot be fully excluded
by this experimental design alone.

**[4]** Octopus cognition is further complicated by the species' notably
short lifespan—most common octopus species live only one to two years,
reproduce once, and die shortly after, a life history pattern
dramatically different from that of long-lived, highly social mammals
such as elephants or chimpanzees, in which sophisticated cognition is
often theorized to have evolved partly to manage multi-year social
relationships and long-term kin recognition. Octopuses are largely
solitary and, with rare exceptions, show little evidence of enduring
social bonds, which has led some researchers to argue that whatever
drove the evolution of octopus cognitive sophistication, it is unlikely
to have been sustained social complexity, pointing instead toward the
demands of predator evasion and prey capture in a soft-bodied animal
with no shell and few other defenses besides behavioral flexibility
and camouflage.

**[5]** This absence of an obvious evolutionary driver analogous to those
proposed for mammalian intelligence has made the octopus a particularly
interesting test case for a broader theoretical question: whether
complex cognition requires any single specific evolutionary pressure, or
whether it can emerge through multiple, quite different evolutionary
pathways in response to quite different ecological problems. If
octopus-like cognitive flexibility evolved primarily in response to
predator evasion and foraging demands in a short-lived, largely solitary
animal, this would suggest that the social-complexity theories developed
to explain primate and cetacean intelligence, however well supported
within those lineages, are not general laws governing the evolution of
intelligence across all of animal life, but rather one pathway among
potentially several.

**[6]** Skeptics of ambitious claims about octopus intelligence caution
that many of the most striking anecdotes—jar-opening, tool-like use of
coconut shells for shelter, apparent play behavior with floating
objects—come from observations of individual animals, often in captivity
under conditions that may themselves induce novel behavior, rather than
from large, controlled, peer-reviewed studies. These critics argue that
without more rigorous comparative work, it remains difficult to
determine how representative such behaviors are of octopus cognition
generally, as opposed to being unusual responses to unusual captive
conditions that would rarely, if ever, arise in the wild—a methodological
caution that applies, to a lesser extent, to comparative cognition
research across many species, not only cephalopods.

## Questions

**1. Vocabulary in context:** The word "autonomy" in paragraph 1 is
closest in meaning to

**2.** According to paragraph 1, what percentage of an octopus's neurons

(A) About one-third
(B) About half
(C) About two-thirds
(D) Nearly all

**3. Vocabulary in context:** The phrase "neural chauvinism" in paragraph
2 refers to
(A) the idea that octopus arms can act independently of the brain
(B) judging a very different cognitive system using standards shaped by
    the study of brains similar to our own
(C) the claim that octopuses are more intelligent than mammals
(D) a technical term for the distribution of neurons across an
    octopus's arms

**4. Inference:** It can be inferred from paragraph 3 that the critics
mentioned there believe
(A) octopuses showed no improvement at all across repeated trials
(B) faster performance on repeated trials does not, by itself, fully
    rule out an explanation based on improved motor skill rather than
    conceptual learning
(C) the maze experiments are more reliable than the container experiments
(D) octopuses cannot learn any task under laboratory conditions

**5.** According to paragraph 4, why do some researchers doubt that
social complexity drove the evolution of octopus cognition?
(A) Octopuses are highly social and form long-term bonds.
(B) Octopuses have unusually long lifespans compared to other
    invertebrates.
(C) Octopuses are largely solitary, short-lived, and show little
    evidence of enduring social relationships.
(D) Octopuses reproduce multiple times across several years.

**6.** The word "this" in paragraph 5 ("This absence of an obvious
evolutionary driver...") refers to
(A) the existence of social-complexity theories for primates

    sophistication, given their solitary, short-lived life history
(C) the discovery of 500 million neurons in the octopus nervous system
(D) the fact that octopuses cannot be studied in captivity

**7. Rhetorical purpose:** Why does the author mention elephants and
chimpanzees in paragraph 4?

(C) To prove that all intelligent animals must be long-lived

**8. Insert-text:** Look at the following sentence: *"This caution does
not mean the behaviors are not real, only that their generality remains

**9.** Which of the following best describes the overall relationship
between paragraph 5 and paragraph 6?
(A) Paragraph 6 provides experimental proof of the theory proposed in
    paragraph 5.
(B) Paragraph 6 introduces a methodological caution that tempers how
    confidently the broader theoretical claims in paragraph 5 should be
    accepted.
(C) Paragraph 6 contradicts paragraph 5 directly and shows it to be false.
(D) Paragraph 6 is unrelated to the discussion in paragraph 5.

**10. Prose Summary:** An introductory sentence is provided: *"Octopus
cognition challenges assumptions developed from studying vertebrate
intelligence."* Select the **THREE** answer choices that express the
most important ideas in the passage.

(A) Octopus neurons are mostly distributed across the arms rather than
    centralized in the brain, complicating comparisons to vertebrate
    cognition.
(B) Octopuses' solitary, short-lived life history suggests their
    cognitive abilities likely did not evolve primarily to manage
    long-term social relationships.
(C) All researchers agree that octopuses are more intelligent than
    dogs and crows.
(D) Critics caution that striking captive behaviors may not be
    representative of octopus cognition generally, given limited
    controlled study.
(E) Octopuses are known to live for several decades, longer than most
    mammals studied for intelligence.
(F) Octopuses have no documented ability to learn new tasks under
    laboratory conditions.
