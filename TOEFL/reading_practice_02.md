**Timer: 18 minutes. This passage is more argumentative than Practice
1's narrative structure — it presents competing scholarly positions, which
is where TOEFL Reading's inference and rhetorical-purpose questions get
noticeably harder than factual-recall questions.**

## The Great Oxidation Event and the Origin of Complex Life

**[1]** Roughly 2.4 billion years ago, Earth's atmosphere underwent a
transformation now referred to as the Great Oxidation Event (GOE), during
which free oxygen, previously a trace component of the atmosphere,
accumulated to geologically significant levels for the first time. The
proximate cause is generally agreed upon: cyanobacteria, which had evolved
oxygenic photosynthesis hundreds of millions of years earlier, gradually
produced oxygen faster than it could be absorbed by reactive surface
minerals and dissolved iron in the oceans, which had previously acted as
an oxygen sink. Once these sinks were saturated, atmospheric oxygen began
to rise sharply, leaving a distinctive geological signature in rock
formations from this period.

**[2]** What remains contested is why the rise took so long. Cyanobacteria
are believed to have existed for several hundred million years before the
GOE without producing a comparable atmospheric shift, a delay some
researchers term the "oxygen overshoot problem." One hypothesis holds that
early oxygen production was simply too low in volume, with cyanobacteria
populations constrained by limited nutrient availability, particularly
phosphorus, in the early oceans; on this view, the GOE reflects a gradual
biological scaling-up rather than any sudden change in the chemistry of
the Earth itself. A competing hypothesis instead locates the cause in
geology rather than biology, proposing that the gradual reduction of
volcanic and metamorphic gases capable of consuming oxygen — rather than
any change in oxygen production — eventually allowed oxygen produced at a
roughly constant rate to finally accumulate.

**[3]** The consequences of the GOE for the planet's existing life were
severe. The sudden abundance of a highly reactive gas is thought to have
caused a mass extinction among anaerobic organisms for which oxygen was
directly toxic, an event sometimes termed the "oxygen catastrophe." Some
researchers go further, linking the GOE to one of the most severe ice
ages in Earth's history, the Huronian glaciation, on the theory that
rising oxygen reacted with atmospheric methane — a potent greenhouse gas
on which the early Earth's warmth had substantially depended — and
removed it from the atmosphere, triggering a prolonged global cooling.
This causal link, while widely cited, is not universally accepted; critics
note that the exact timing alignment between the completion of the GOE
and the onset of glaciation remains difficult to establish with the
precision the hypothesis requires, given the limitations of dating
methods for rock this old.

**[4]** Despite these short-term catastrophic effects, the long-term
consequence of the GOE is generally regarded as a necessary, if not
sufficient, precondition for the later evolution of complex life. Aerobic
respiration, which extracts dramatically more usable energy from organic
molecules than any anaerobic metabolic pathway, became possible only once
atmospheric oxygen reached sufficient levels, and this energetic surplus
is widely considered essential to supporting the metabolic demands of
large, multicellular organisms. However, a substantial gap remains between
the GOE and the appearance of the first complex multicellular life in the
fossil record — on the order of 1.5 to 2 billion years — a delay too long
to be explained by oxygen levels alone, since atmospheric oxygen is
believed to have remained relatively low and fluctuating for much of this
intervening period, a stretch of time sometimes called the "boring
billion" for the apparent scarcity of major evolutionary innovation
during it.

**[5]** Resolving why complex life did not emerge sooner, once oxygen was
available at all, has become its own area of active research, with
proposed explanations ranging from insufficient oxygen concentration
during the "boring billion" to the time required for the evolutionary
innovation of the eukaryotic cell itself, which many researchers consider
a more fundamental bottleneck than atmospheric chemistry. Proponents of
this latter view point out that prokaryotic cells, which dominated Earth
for billions of years before and after the GOE, appear to lack the
internal structural complexity — such as membrane-bound organelles —
that would have been needed to scale up to large multicellular body plans
regardless of how much oxygen was available to them.

## Questions

**1. Vocabulary in context:** The word "proximate" in paragraph 1 is
closest in meaning to
(A) approximate (B) immediate/direct (C) ancient (D) disputed

**2.** According to paragraph 1, why did atmospheric oxygen not rise
immediately once cyanobacteria began producing it?
(A) Cyanobacteria produced too little oxygen to matter
(B) Reactive minerals and dissolved iron absorbed the oxygen until saturated
(C) Volcanic gases immediately destroyed all oxygen produced
(D) Early oxygen was chemically different from modern oxygen

**3.** The two hypotheses presented in paragraph 2 primarily disagree about
(A) whether the GOE happened at all
(B) whether the cause of delayed oxygen accumulation was biological or geological
(C) whether cyanobacteria existed before the GOE
(D) the exact date of the GOE

**4. Inference:** It can be inferred from paragraph 2 that, under the
"nutrient limitation" hypothesis, the amount of oxygen cyanobacteria
could produce was constrained mainly by
(A) a lack of sunlight reaching the ocean
(B) the availability of certain nutrients, such as phosphorus
(C) competition from early animal life
(D) the gradual cooling of the early atmosphere

**5.** Why does the author mention the "Huronian glaciation" in paragraph 3?
(A) To provide an example of an ice age entirely unrelated to the GOE
(B) To present a disputed causal hypothesis linking the GOE to a major climate event
(C) To prove that methane has never significantly affected Earth's climate
(D) To establish the exact date of the GOE beyond doubt

**6.** According to paragraph 3, what is one criticism of the methane-removal
hypothesis for the Huronian glaciation?
(A) Methane is not actually a greenhouse gas
(B) Oxygen does not react with methane under any conditions
(C) Precisely aligning the timing of the GOE and the glaciation is difficult given dating limitations
(D) The Huronian glaciation is now known never to have occurred

**7. Insert-text:** Look at the following sentence: *"This suggests that
oxygen availability, while necessary, was not the limiting factor during
at least part of this interval."* Where would it best fit in paragraph 4?
(Identify the sentence it should follow.)

**8.** The phrase "a more fundamental bottleneck" in paragraph 5 refers to
(A) the total volume of atmospheric oxygen during the GOE
(B) the evolutionary development of the eukaryotic cell
(C) the extinction of anaerobic organisms
(D) the rate of volcanic gas emission

**9. Rhetorical purpose:** Why does the author present both the "nutrient
limitation" and "geological" hypotheses in paragraph 2, and both the
"oxygen concentration" and "eukaryotic cell" explanations in paragraph 5,
rather than asserting a single accepted explanation in each case?
(A) To demonstrate that scientists have abandoned studying the GOE entirely
(B) To accurately represent genuine, unresolved scientific disagreement on causation
(C) Because the two hypotheses in each pair are known to be identical
(D) To argue that none of the proposed explanations has any supporting evidence

**10. Prose Summary:** An introductory sentence is provided: *"The Great
Oxidation Event reshaped Earth's atmosphere, with consequences and causes
that remain partly unresolved."* Select the **THREE** answer choices that
express the most important ideas in the passage.

(A) Cyanobacteria's oxygenic photosynthesis was the proximate cause of
    rising atmospheric oxygen, though why the rise was delayed remains debated.
(B) The GOE caused a mass extinction of anaerobic organisms and is linked,
    though not without dispute, to a major ice age.
(C) Modern eukaryotic cells first evolved immediately after the GOE concluded.
(D) A large time gap between the GOE and complex multicellular life suggests
    oxygen availability alone does not fully explain the timing of complex life's emergence.
(E) Volcanic gases have never played any role in Earth's atmospheric chemistry.
(F) The Huronian glaciation has been definitively proven to have no relationship to the GOE.
