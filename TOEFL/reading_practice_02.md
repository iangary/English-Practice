## The Spread of Agriculture in Prehistoric Europe

**[1]** For much of the twentieth century, archaeologists debated how
agriculture spread from its origins in the Fertile Crescent, roughly
10,000 years ago, into Europe over the following several thousand years.
Two competing models dominated the discussion. The "demic diffusion"
model proposed that farming spread primarily through the migration of
farming populations themselves, who gradually expanded into new
territory, displacing or absorbing existing hunter-gatherer populations
as they went. The competing "cultural diffusion" model proposed instead
that farming technology and knowledge spread primarily through contact
and exchange between neighboring groups, with existing hunter-gatherer
populations adopting agricultural practices from their farming neighbors
without substantial population movement or replacement.

**[2]** Early evidence seemed difficult to reconcile decisively between
the two models. The archaeological record showed a clear directional
spread of farming practices—pottery styles, domesticated crop remains,
and permanent settlement patterns—moving from southeastern Europe
northwestward over roughly two millennia, consistent with either model
equally well, since both migration and the spread of ideas would produce
a similar directional pattern in material culture left behind. Skeletal
evidence offered some clues but was often ambiguous, since skeletal
features can be influenced by diet and lifestyle changes independent of
ancestry, making it difficult to distinguish a population that adopted
farming from a population that was replaced by farmers using skeletal
analysis alone.

**[3]** Ancient DNA analysis, which became feasible at scale only in the
2010s, has substantially clarified the picture, lending considerably more
support to the demic diffusion model than many archaeologists had
expected. Genetic studies comparing the DNA of early European farmers to
that of the hunter-gatherer populations that preceded them in the same
regions found that early farmers were genetically much more similar to
Anatolian populations from the region of the agricultural origin than to
local European hunter-gatherers, indicating substantial population
migration rather than purely local adoption of new practices. This
finding was consistent across multiple independent sites throughout
Europe, which researchers considered important, since a single site's
unusual result might reflect a local anomaly rather than a continental
pattern.

**[4]** However, subsequent, more detailed genetic research has
complicated what might otherwise have seemed a clean victory for the
demic diffusion model. While early farming populations across much of
Europe did show predominantly Anatolian ancestry, the proportion of
hunter-gatherer ancestry in farming populations was found to increase
gradually over subsequent centuries in many regions, suggesting ongoing
genetic mixing between farmers and surviving hunter-gatherer groups
rather than complete replacement in a single wave. This pattern varied
considerably by region: in some areas, hunter-gatherer ancestry rebounded
substantially within a few centuries, while in others, the predominantly
Anatolian genetic signature persisted far longer with comparatively
little admixture, a regional variation that remains only partially
explained.

**[5]** Current scholarship generally characterizes the spread of
agriculture into Europe as involving substantial migration, consistent
with the demic diffusion model's core claim, but one that unfolded
through a more prolonged and regionally variable process of interaction
and mixing with existing populations than the original, simpler version
of either competing model proposed. Some researchers now describe the
genetic evidence as supporting a "hybrid" model, though this label has
been criticized by others as potentially obscuring important regional
differences by implying a single, uniform process occurred continent-wide
when the evidence instead points to meaningfully different trajectories
in different regions.

## Questions

**1. Vocabulary in context:** The word "reconcile" in paragraph 2 is
closest in meaning to
(A) repair (B) make compatible (C) remember (D) discover

**2.** According to paragraph 1, the demic diffusion model proposed that
farming spread primarily through
(A) the exchange of ideas between neighboring groups
(B) the migration of farming populations into new territory
(C) climate change forcing hunter-gatherers to adopt new diets
(D) trade routes established for non-agricultural goods

**3.** Why does the author state that skeletal evidence was often
"ambiguous" in paragraph 2?
(A) Because no skeletal remains from this period have ever been found
(B) Because skeletal features can be affected by diet/lifestyle
    independent of ancestry, making population replacement hard to detect
(C) Because skeletal evidence always supports the cultural diffusion
    model
(D) Because archaeologists refused to study skeletal remains

**4. Inference:** It can be inferred from paragraph 2 that, before ancient
DNA analysis became available, the directional spread pattern seen in
the archaeological record
(A) definitively proved the demic diffusion model correct
(B) definitively proved the cultural diffusion model correct
(C) could not by itself distinguish between the two competing models
(D) was inconsistent with both models equally

**5.** According to paragraph 3, why did researchers consider it
important that the genetic finding was consistent across multiple
independent sites?
(A) It ruled out the possibility that a single site's result was a local
    anomaly rather than a broader pattern
(B) It proved that no hunter-gatherers survived anywhere in Europe
(C) It showed that Anatolian populations never actually migrated
(D) It contradicted the archaeological pottery evidence entirely

**6.** The word "admixture" in paragraph 4 most likely refers to
(A) a type of pottery style
(B) genetic mixing between two previously distinct populations
(C) a climate pattern affecting crop growth
(D) a method of radiocarbon dating

**7. Inference:** It can be inferred from paragraph 4 that the regional
variation in hunter-gatherer ancestry rebound
(A) has been completely and satisfactorily explained by current research
(B) is not yet fully understood, according to the passage
(C) only occurred in southeastern Europe
(D) disproves the demic diffusion model entirely

**8.** Why does the author mention criticism of the "hybrid" model label
in paragraph 5?
(A) To argue that the hybrid model should be abandoned entirely
(B) To note a limitation some researchers see in using a single uniform
    label when regional trajectories actually differed
(C) To prove that demic diffusion was completely wrong
(D) To introduce a third, entirely unrelated model

**9. Rhetorical purpose:** What is the overall rhetorical structure of the
passage?
(A) It presents two competing models, describes evidence that initially
    favored one, then shows how further evidence complicated a simple
    conclusion
(B) It presents one model and systematically disproves it
(C) It argues that archaeological evidence is always more reliable than
    genetic evidence
(D) It describes a single model that has remained unchanged since the
    1970s

**10. Prose Summary:** An introductory sentence is provided: *"Genetic
evidence has reshaped the debate over how agriculture spread into
Europe."* Select the **THREE** answer choices that express the most
important ideas in the passage.

(A) Ancient DNA analysis showed early European farmers were genetically
    closer to Anatolian populations than to local hunter-gatherers.
(B) Hunter-gatherer ancestry increased gradually in many regions after
    the initial farming migration, suggesting ongoing population mixing.
(C) Skeletal evidence alone definitively proved the cultural diffusion
    model correct decades before DNA analysis existed.
(D) The regional pattern of ancestry mixing varied considerably and
    remains only partially explained.
(E) All European regions show an identical, uniform genetic pattern with
    no meaningful variation.
(F) Agriculture originated independently in at least four regions of
    Europe simultaneously.
