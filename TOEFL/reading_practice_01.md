## The Domestication of the Horse

**[1]** For much of the twentieth century, archaeologists debated where
and when horses were first domesticated, relying primarily on indirect
evidence such as changes in bone structure and the presence of horse
remains at human settlements. The breakthrough came from an unexpected
source: dental and genetic analysis of horse remains from the Botai
culture of present-day Kazakhstan, dated to roughly 3500 BCE. Researchers
identified wear patterns on horse teeth consistent with bit use and found
lipid residues in ceramic vessels matching mare's milk, suggesting that
Botai horses were not merely hunted but managed, ridden, and milked.

**[2]** This finding initially appeared to settle the question: the Botai
were crowned, in popular accounts, as the first horse domesticators, and
all modern domestic horses were assumed to descend from their herds.
However, a 2018 genomic study complicated this narrative considerably. By
sequencing ancient DNA from Botai horse remains and comparing it to
modern domestic horses, researchers found that Botai horses were
genetically distinct from virtually all horses alive today. Remarkably,
the study revealed that Botai horses were the ancestors not of modern
domestic breeds but of the Przewalski's horse, long assumed to be the
last truly wild horse population on Earth. The implication was striking:
Przewalski's horses are not wild in the sense previously believed, but
are instead feral descendants of a domestication event that did not lead
to today's domestic horses at all.

**[3]** If Botai horses were a domestication dead end, the origin of
modern domestic horses remained unresolved until a far larger genomic
study published in 2021 analyzed DNA from 273 ancient horses across
Eurasia. This research pointed to the Western Eurasian steppe, in the
region of the lower Volga and Don rivers, as the likely origin of modern
domestic horses, with a rapid expansion beginning around 2200 BCE. The
speed of this expansion was itself significant: within a few centuries,
horses with this genetic signature had almost entirely replaced other
regional horse populations across a vast area stretching from Western
Europe to Mongolia, a spread the researchers linked to the simultaneous
emergence of spoke-wheeled chariots and new forms of mounted warfare.

**[4]** The near-total replacement of prior horse lineages raises
questions that remain contested. One hypothesis holds that horses from
this lineage possessed specific genetic traits—including variants
associated with a calmer temperament and a stronger back suited to
riding—that made them so much more useful for transport and warfare that
other lineages were rapidly displaced through deliberate breeding
preference. A competing hypothesis emphasizes cultural and economic
factors over biology: as mounted warfare and trade networks expanding
along the steppe made horse exchange highly valuable, populations with
early access to superior animals may have out-competed neighboring groups
economically and militarily, regardless of whether the genetic advantages
were as large as proposed.

**[5]** Resolving this debate has implications beyond archaeology. The
spread of horse-based mobility is widely credited with reshaping Bronze
Age Eurasia, enabling the long-distance movement of people, goods, and
languages at a scale not previously possible. Some linguists have linked
this expansion to the spread of Indo-European languages, though this
connection remains debated and is complicated by the difficulty of
aligning genetic, archaeological, and linguistic timelines precisely. What
is less disputed is that the horse's transition from a hunted animal to a
managed one ranks among the most consequential developments in human
history, comparable in scale to the domestication of cattle or the
adoption of agriculture, yet arrived at considerably later and through a
more circuitous path than earlier theories assumed.

## Questions

**1. Vocabulary in context:** The word "circuitous" in paragraph 5 is
closest in meaning to
(A) deliberate (B) indirect (C) rapid (D) controversial

**2.** According to paragraph 1, what evidence suggested that Botai
horses were managed rather than simply hunted?
(A) Cave paintings depicting riders
(B) Tooth wear and milk residue in pottery
(C) Written records from early Kazakh settlements
(D) Comparison with modern horse breeds

**3.** The word "this narrative" in paragraph 2 refers to
(A) the idea that Przewalski's horses are truly wild
(B) the idea that the Botai were the ancestors of all modern horses
(C) the idea that horses were milked before being ridden
(D) the idea that genomic studies are more reliable than dental studies

**4. Inference:** It can be inferred from paragraph 2 that, before the
2018 genomic study, most scientists believed
(A) Przewalski's horses were a feral, not truly wild, population
(B) Botai horses were unrelated to any horses alive today
(C) Przewalski's horses represented a population never domesticated
(D) the Botai never actually rode horses

**5.** According to paragraph 3, the rapid spread of the Volga-Don horse
lineage has been linked to
(A) a shift toward a plant-based diet among steppe populations
(B) the invention of spoke-wheeled chariots and new mounted warfare
(C) a change in climate that eliminated other horse populations
(D) the migration of the Botai culture itself

**6.** Which of the following is presented in paragraph 4 as a
competing explanation to the "genetic advantage" hypothesis?
(A) Other horse lineages went extinct due to disease.
(B) Economic and military advantage from early access to good horses
    drove displacement, regardless of genetic difference.
(C) Horses from the Volga-Don lineage were not actually more numerous.
(D) The displacement never actually occurred and is a modern myth.

**7. Insert-text:** Look at the following sentence: *"This makes the
genetic evidence alone insufficient to settle the debate."* Where would
it best fit in paragraph 4? (Mark the position — after which sentence?)

**8.** Why does the author mention "the spread of Indo-European
languages" in paragraph 5?
(A) To argue that linguistics has definitively solved the debate
(B) To illustrate one further-reaching, though contested, implication
    of the horse domestication timeline
(C) To contradict the genomic findings described earlier
(D) To introduce a new archaeological culture not previously discussed

**9. Rhetorical purpose:** Why does the author include two separate
genomic studies (2018 and 2021) rather than just the more recent one?
(A) To show that the field's conclusions overturned each other as new,
    more comprehensive evidence became available
(B) To prove that genomic studies are generally unreliable
(C) Because the 2018 study has since been fully retracted
(D) To compare Kazakhstan and the Volga-Don region economically

**10. Prose Summary:** An introductory sentence is provided: *"Genetic
research has substantially revised earlier theories of horse
domestication."* Select the **THREE** answer choices that express the
most important ideas in the passage.

(A) Botai horses, once thought to be the ancestors of all modern domestic
    horses, turned out to be ancestors of Przewalski's horses instead.
(B) Modern domestic horses likely originated in the Western Eurasian
    steppe around 2200 BCE and spread rapidly across Eurasia.
(C) Cave art is the primary form of evidence used to study horse
    domestication.
(D) The reasons for the rapid replacement of older horse lineages —
    genetic versus cultural/economic — remain debated.
(E) Horses were domesticated independently in at least six distinct
    regions of the world.
(F) The Botai culture is located in modern-day Mongolia.

## Answer Key

1. **B** (indirect) 2. **B** 3. **B** 4. **C** 5. **B** 6. **B**
7. Best placed directly after the sentence ending "...out-competed
