# TOEFL Listening — Drill Set

**Protocol:** Since this is a text-based training ground, "listen" by
reading each passage **exactly once, at normal reading speed, out loud or
sub-vocalized, without stopping or re-reading** — that's the real
constraint the test imposes (audio plays once, no rewind), not the
specific medium. Take notes while you go, exactly as you would in the
exam. Then cover the passage and answer the questions from your notes
only. This is non-negotiable: answering while still able to see the
passage defeats the entire point of the drill.

**Timer:** 3 minutes reading-aloud-once + note-taking, then 2 minutes to
answer the 5 questions per passage, no looking back.

## Note-taking framework (use this structure, don't freehand it)

```
TOPIC: ___________
MAIN POINT: ___________
Supporting point 1: ___
Supporting point 2: ___
Supporting point 3: ___
SIGNAL WORDS heard (however/but/therefore/for example/the problem is):
___
```

Capture structure (what connects to what, and the speaker's attitude),
not transcription. If you're writing more than 5–6 words per supporting
point, you're transcribing, not note-taking — that habit is the single
biggest cause of missing the next point while still writing the last one.

## Passage 1: Academic Lecture (Biology)

> "Today we're talking about bioluminescence — the production of light
> by living organisms. Now, most people, when they think of
> bioluminescence, think of fireflies, and that's a fine example, but it's
> actually far more common in the ocean than on land. In fact, researchers
> estimate that the majority of deep-sea organisms — somewhere around
> 75 to 90 percent, depending on the depth — produce some form of light.
> Why would that be useful in an environment with no sunlight at all?
> Well, there are at least three distinct functions. First, predation —
> the anglerfish is the classic example, using a glowing lure to attract
> prey directly into its mouth. Second, defense — some species of squid
> release a cloud of glowing particles instead of ink, essentially a
> decoy, when threatened, buying themselves a few seconds to escape while
> the predator is confused by the light. And third — and this one surprises
> people — communication. Certain species of deep-sea fish use specific
> light patterns, almost like a code, to identify potential mates of the
> same species in total darkness where visual recognition would otherwise
> be impossible. Now here's the interesting part: the light itself comes
> from a chemical reaction, not electricity — a molecule called luciferin
> reacts with oxygen, catalyzed by an enzyme called luciferase, and that
> reaction releases energy as light rather than heat, which is actually
> remarkably efficient compared to, say, an incandescent bulb, which wastes
> most of its energy as heat. Some organisms produce this light
> themselves; others, interestingly, don't produce it at all but instead
> host bioluminescent bacteria in specialized organs — a symbiotic
> relationship where the bacteria get a safe home and a steady food
> supply, and the host gets light on demand."

**Questions:**
1. What is the lecture mainly about?
2. According to the lecture, what percentage of deep-sea organisms are
   estimated to produce light?
3. What is the function of the glowing lure used by the anglerfish?
4. Why is the squid's glowing particle cloud compared to a decoy?
5. How do some organisms produce light without generating it themselves?

<details>
<summary>Answer key (reveal after answering)</summary>

1. The phenomenon of bioluminescence, particularly its prevalence and
   functions in deep-sea organisms.
2. Roughly 75–90% (depending on depth).
3. Predation — attracting prey directly to the anglerfish's mouth.
4. It distracts/confuses the predator, giving the squid time to escape,
   similar to how a decoy diverts attention from the real target.
5. Through a symbiotic relationship — hosting bioluminescent bacteria in
   specialized organs instead of producing the light themselves.
</details>

## Passage 2: Campus Conversation (Student + Academic Advisor)

> **Student:** "Hi, I was hoping you could help me — I'm trying to
> figure out if I should switch my minor from Economics to Statistics.
> I'm a Psychology major, and I keep hearing that Statistics would be
> more useful for grad school applications."
>
> **Advisor:** "That's a reasonable thing to weigh. Can I ask — do you
> know yet whether you want to go into a research-focused graduate
> program, or something more clinical, like counseling?"
>
> **Student:** "Honestly, I'm leaning research. I really liked the
> research methods course I took last semester."
>
> **Advisor:** "Okay, that changes my answer quite a bit. If you're
> leaning research, Statistics is going to serve you better almost across
> the board — most competitive research-focused psychology programs
> specifically look for statistical coursework beyond the one intro stats
> class that's already required for the Psych major. Economics is a fine
> minor, but it won't signal the same thing to an admissions committee
> looking at a research applicant."
>
> **Student:** "That makes sense. The one thing I'm worried about is
> timing — I've already taken two Economics courses toward the minor.
> Would those just be wasted?"
>
> **Advisor:** "Not entirely — they'd count as free electives, so you
> wouldn't lose the credits, just the minor designation. The real
> question is whether you have room for three more Statistics courses in
> the time you have left. Which year are you?"
>
> **Student:** "I'm a sophomore, so I'd have plenty of time, actually."
>
> **Advisor:** "Then I'd say switch. Being a sophomore is exactly the
> scenario where this is an easy decision — if you were a second-semester
> junior, I'd probably tell you to stick with what you've already got
> most of the credits for instead."

**Questions:**
1. Why does the student visit the advisor?
2. What does the advisor ask the student before giving advice, and why
   does that question matter?
3. What will happen to the two Economics courses the student already
   completed if they switch minors?
4. What is the advisor's final recommendation, and what key fact makes
   it an "easy decision" according to the advisor?

<details>
<summary>Answer key (reveal after answering)</summary>

1. To decide whether to switch her minor from Economics to Statistics.
2. Whether she wants a research-focused or clinical graduate program —
   it matters because the right minor depends on her career direction
   (Statistics signals research readiness; it wouldn't matter as much for
   a clinical path).
3. They won't be lost — they'll count as free electives, just not toward
   a minor.
4. Switch to Statistics; the key fact is that she's only a sophomore, so
   she has enough time left to complete the Statistics minor requirements
   (the advisor implies a junior would get different advice).
</details>

## Passage 3: Academic Lecture (Economics) — harder, longer, two intertwined arguments

> "Let's talk about a concept called the 'paradox of thrift.' On an
> individual level, saving money is, obviously, a good thing — it's
> prudent, it builds a cushion, nobody would argue against an individual
> household saving more. But here's the paradox: if an entire economy
> simultaneously decides to save more and spend less — say, during a
> recession when everyone gets nervous at once — that collective decision
> can actually make the economy worse off, not better. Here's the
> mechanism. Your spending is someone else's income. If you cut your
> spending to save more, the business you would have bought from earns
> less. That business, in turn, may cut wages, or lay someone off, or
> delay an investment. That laid-off worker now has less income, so they
> cut their spending too, and the cycle continues outward. The end result
> can be that aggregate income across the whole economy falls by more
> than the amount anyone intended to save — and in the worst cases,
> because everyone's income has fallen, total actual savings across the
> economy can end up *lower* than before everyone tried to save more, not
> higher. That's the paradox: an individually rational action, taken
> collectively, undermines its own goal. Now, I want to be careful here,
> because this idea, which comes from Keynesian economics, is genuinely
> contested — it's not a universally accepted law of economics the way,
> say, supply and demand curves are. Critics, generally from a more
> classical or Austrian economics tradition, argue that the paradox
> assumes savings just sit idle, doing nothing, when in reality, in a
> functioning financial system, saved money gets lent out as credit,
> which funds investment by other businesses — so the spending doesn't
> actually disappear from the economy, it's just redirected rather than
> destroyed. Keynesians respond that this redirection mechanism breaks
> down specifically during a liquidity trap or a banking crisis, when
> banks themselves become unwilling or unable to lend even when savings
> are available — which is exactly the scenario, they'd point out, where
> the paradox of thrift is most likely to actually bite in the real
> world."

**Questions:**
1. What is the paradox of thrift, stated in one sentence?
2. Walk through the mechanism the lecturer describes, step by step, for
   how one household's increased saving can reduce the whole economy's
   income.
3. What is the critics' (classical/Austrian) counterargument, and what is
   its core assumption about saved money?
4. According to the lecturer, under what specific condition do Keynesians
   say the critics' counterargument breaks down?
5. Does the lecturer present the paradox of thrift as a settled,
   universally accepted fact? Cite the specific phrase used.

<details>
<summary>Answer key (reveal after answering)</summary>

1. An action that is rational for an individual (saving more) can, if
   adopted by an entire economy at once, make the economy collectively
   worse off and even reduce total savings.
2. Household cuts spending → business earns less → business cuts wages/
   jobs/investment → laid-off or lower-paid workers cut their own
   spending → the cycle repeats and spreads, shrinking aggregate income
   economy-wide by more than the original intended increase in saving.
3. Saved money doesn't sit idle — it gets lent out as credit and funds
   investment elsewhere, so spending power is redirected, not destroyed,
   meaning the paradox's core assumption (that saving removes money from
   the economy) doesn't hold in a normally functioning financial system.
4. During a liquidity trap or banking crisis, when banks are unwilling or
   unable to lend out the available savings.
5. No — he explicitly says it is "genuinely contested" and "not a
   universally accepted law of economics the way... supply and demand
   curves are."
</details>

## Self-audit after each passage

- Did you answer from notes alone, with zero re-reading of the passage?
  If you peeked, the drill doesn't count — redo it with a passage you
  haven't seen.
- For the conversation: did you capture *why* the advisor's recommendation
  changed based on the student's answer, or only the final recommendation
  itself? TOEFL frequently tests the reasoning chain, not just the
  conclusion.
- For Passage 3: could you reconstruct both sides of the debate from your
  notes, or only the lecturer's main point? Passages with an embedded
  counterargument are the hardest note-taking case — practice flagging
  "but/critics say/however" moments explicitly as you take notes.

Log a self-estimated score (X/5 or X/4 per passage) and whether your
miss was a **note-taking failure** (didn't capture it) vs. a
**comprehension failure** (captured it, misunderstood it) in
`PROGRESS_LOG.md` — these need different fixes, same as Quant.

### Scoring
5/5 (or 4/4) consistently across passage types = on pace for 24+/30 on
Listening. Missing specifically on the "why/reasoning" questions, not the
"what" questions, means your note-taking is capturing facts but not
logical connectors — go back through this file's transcripts and
underline every signal word (however, because, the reason is, in
