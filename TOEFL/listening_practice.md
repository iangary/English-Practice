## Passage 1 — Lecture (Biology)

> "Let's continue with plant defense mechanisms. Today's focus is on
> induced defenses — responses plants activate only after being attacked,
> as opposed to constitutive defenses, which are always present, like a
> tree's bark or a cactus's spines. One of the clearest examples of an
> induced defense is in wild tobacco plants. When caterpillars begin
> feeding on the leaves, the plant detects specific chemical compounds in
> the caterpillar's saliva — not just the physical damage from chewing,
> which is an important distinction, because the plant can actually tell
> the difference between damage from an insect bite and damage from, say,
> a pair of scissors cutting the same leaf. In response to the saliva
> compounds specifically, the plant releases volatile organic compounds
> into the air. These volatiles serve two purposes simultaneously: first,
> they directly repel some caterpillar species, and second — and this is
> the part that surprised researchers initially — they attract parasitic
> wasps that lay their eggs inside the caterpillars, which eventually
> kills the caterpillar from the inside. So the plant isn't just
> defending itself directly; it's recruiting a third party, a predator of
> its attacker, to do additional damage control. There's an evolutionary
> cost to this strategy, though: producing these compounds requires
> energy the plant could otherwise use for growth or seed production,
> which is likely why the response is induced rather than constant — it's
> only 'switched on' when the cost is justified by an active threat."

**Questions 1–5 (multiple choice unless noted):**

1. What is the main difference between constitutive and induced defenses?
 (A) Induced defenses are stronger (B) Constitutive defenses are always present; induced ones activate on attack (C) Constitutive defenses only work against insects

2. How does the tobacco plant distinguish caterpillar damage from mechanical damage (e.g., scissors)?
 (A) By the amount of leaf removed (B) By detecting compounds in the caterpillar's saliva (C) By the time of day the damage occurs

3. (Select TWO) What do the released volatile compounds do?
 (A) Repel some caterpillars (B) Heal the damaged leaf tissue (C) Attract parasitic wasps (D) Increase seed production

4. According to the lecture, why is the defense "induced" rather than constant?
 (A) It would harm the plant's own leaves (B) Producing the compounds has an energy cost (C) Caterpillars would become immune to it

5. What is the lecturer's purpose in mentioning the parasitic wasps?
 (A) To show the plant recruits a third party against its attacker (B) To criticize this defense strategy as inefficient (C) To introduce an unrelated species

## Passage 2 — Conversation (Student Services)

> **Student:** "Hi, I'm trying to figure out if I can switch my housing
> contract from the double room I'm in now to a single — my roommate
> situation isn't working out."
> **Advisor:** "Okay, let's see what's available. Mid-semester room
> changes are possible, but there's a process. First, have you talked to
> your roommate and filed anything with your Resident Advisor? We
> generally require that step before approving a transfer, mainly because
> a lot of conflicts get resolved once there's a mediated conversation."
> **Student:** "We talked, it didn't really go anywhere. I haven't filed
> anything formal though."
> **Advisor:** "Okay, so that's step one — go file the incident report
> with your RA this week. Once that's on record, you'd fill out a room
> change request form, and then it goes on a waitlist, since singles are
> in high demand and we're at about 95% occupancy this semester. Typical
> wait is two to four weeks, but it can be longer."
> **Student:** "That's a long time if things stay tense."
> **Advisor:** "I hear you. There is one other option — if the conflict
> is serious enough, you can request an expedited review, which skips the
> waitlist, but that requires documentation of the conflict's severity,
> which is really what the RA incident report would provide. So honestly,
> filing that report properly isn't just a formality — it's what would
> actually get you considered for the faster track, if it comes to that."
> **Student:** "Okay, that changes things — I'll go file it today, then."
> **Advisor:** "Good. And one more thing: there's a $75 administrative
> fee for room changes initiated after week 3 of the semester, which
> we're past now — but that fee is waived if the transfer is
> conflict-related rather than just a preference change, so make sure
> that's noted on your form."

**Questions 6–9:**

6. Why does the student visit Student Services?
 (A) To complain about noise (B) To request a change from a double to a single room (C) To report a billing error

7. What does the advisor say is generally required before approving a room transfer?
 (A) Payment of a fee (B) A filed RA incident report after talking to the roommate (C) Approval from both roommates' parents

8. What does the student realize by the end of the conversation?
 (A) The room change is impossible (B) Filing the report could also get her an expedited review (C) She needs a new roommate first

9. Why might the $75 fee be waived in this case?
 (A) The student is a first-year (B) The transfer is conflict-related, not a preference change (C) The dorm is under capacity
